## What the Odyssey is

The *Odyssey* is a poem of roughly twelve thousand one hundred lines
in dactylic hexameter, divided in the transmitted text into
twenty-four books. Its subject, announced in its first line, is a man:
*ándra moi énnepe, Moûsa, polýtropon* — "tell me, Muse, of the man of
many turns." The adjective is the poem's first difficulty. *Polytropos*
means turned many ways, and it can be praise or accusation: widely
travelled, or shifty. The poem never resolves the ambiguity, and
readers who want a straightforward hero have been arguing with the
first line for two and a half thousand years.

What follows is the story of a return. Odysseus, king of Ithaca, has
been away twenty years — ten at Troy and ten getting home. The poem
begins in the last of those years, with the hero held on a distant
island, his son grown, his father withdrawn to the country, his wife
besieged by suitors who are eating his estate while they wait for her
to choose one of them, and his household staff quietly dividing into
the loyal and the disloyal. Over twenty-four books he gets home,
conceals himself, tests everyone, and takes the house back by killing
every man in it.

It is not principally an adventure story, though it contains the most
famous adventures in European literature. The monsters occupy four
books of twenty-four. The other twenty are about a household, a
kingdom, a marriage, a son, a set of obligations between hosts and
guests, and the question of what a man is when nobody knows who he is.

## Traditional authorship

The poem is attributed to Homer. That attribution is ancient, nearly
universal in antiquity, and not verifiable. Whether one poet composed
both Homeric epics was already disputed in antiquity — the ancient
scholars who separated them were called the *chorizontes* — and the
modern argument about composition, unity and transmission has run
since 1795. This platform treats the attribution as traditional rather
than established, and sets out the state of the question at
<a href="/homeric-question">The Homeric Question</a>.

What is not in doubt is that the poem stands at the end of a long oral
tradition. Its language is a *Kunstsprache*, an artificial poetic
dialect nobody ever spoke, built out of layers of different ages and
carrying a formulaic system of remarkable economy. See
<a href="/homer/oral-tradition">Oral tradition and the making of the poems</a>.

## Approximate composition context

The conventional dating is the eighth or early seventh century BCE,
with the *Odyssey* generally held to be the later of the two epics.
The dating is an inference from language, from the material culture
the poem describes, and from datable references — the Phoenician
traders who appear repeatedly belong to the world after about 800 BCE.
It is not a transmitted fact and it is not precise.

The poem's own setting is the aftermath of the Trojan War, which the
Greeks placed in what we call the Late Bronze Age. The distance
between the world the poem describes and the world of the poet is one
of the central problems in reading it, and it cannot be resolved by
choosing one of them. See <a href="/homer-and-history">Homer and history</a>.

## Relationship to the Iliad

The two poems share a world, a metre, a diction and a cast, and they
are almost opposite in temperament.

The *Iliad* is a poem of a few weeks, in one place, about the wrath of
a man who chooses a short glorious life. It ends with a funeral. The
*Odyssey* is a poem of ten years across the known and unknown world,
about a man who wants above all to survive and get home, and it ends
with a household restored. The *Iliad* is concerned with honour and
mortality; the *Odyssey* with endurance, disguise, recognition and
property.

The *Odyssey* also knows the *Iliad*'s story and takes positions on
it. Achilles, met among the dead in Book 11, says he would rather be a
landless labourer among the living than king of all the dead — a
direct repudiation of the value system the *Iliad* is built on, spoken
by the man who embodied it. Agamemnon's homecoming, murdered by his
wife and her lover, is invoked repeatedly as the shadow-version of
Odysseus's own: the return that goes wrong, held up as a warning to
Telemachus and as the reason Odysseus tests his household before
revealing himself.

See <a href="/comparisons/iliad-and-odyssey">Iliad and Odyssey</a>.

## Narrative structure

The poem's architecture is its most sophisticated feature and the one
most often lost in retelling. It falls into four movements:

**Books 1–4, the Telemachy.** Odysseus does not appear. The poem opens
in Ithaca with the crisis in his house and follows his son Telemachus
to Pylos and Sparta in search of news. The effect is to establish what
is at stake before the hero exists on the page.

**Books 5–8, from Ogygia to Scheria.** Odysseus appears in Book 5,
weeping on Calypso's beach. He is released, wrecked, and washed up
among the Phaeacians, who receive him with perfect hospitality.

**Books 9–12, the *Apologoi*.** Odysseus tells his own wanderings, in
the first person, at the Phaeacian court. The Cyclops, Circe, the
dead, the Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, the cattle of the Sun — all of
the poem's famous material is narrated by the character with the
strongest reason to shape a story in his own favour, and the poem
knows this.

**Books 13–24, Ithaca.** The longest movement, and the one popular
memory shortens most. Odysseus lands disguised, lodges with a
swineherd, reveals himself to his son, enters his own house as a
beggar, is abused there, is recognised by an old servant from a scar,
strings the bow, kills the suitors, is tested by his wife, and faces
the consequences.

Beginning in the middle, withholding the hero, and putting the
marvels into retrospective first-person narration are choices. The
poem is constructed, not assembled.

## The journey home

The wanderings occupy Books 9 to 12 and are told by Odysseus at a
banquet. The sequence is: the Cicones, whose city he sacks and where
he loses men to his crew's refusal to leave; the Lotus-Eaters, whose
food destroys the will to return; Polyphemus the Cyclops, blinded by a
trick of language as much as of violence; Aeolus, whose bag of winds
the crew opens in sight of Ithaca; the Laestrygonians, who destroy all
the ships but one; Circe, who turns the men into swine and then
becomes a host; the journey to the edge of the world to consult the
dead; the Sirens; Scylla and Charybdis, where six men are eaten
because there is no better option; Thrinacia, where the crew eat the
cattle of the Sun and are destroyed for it; and Ogygia, where Calypso
holds him seven years.

Two things about this sequence are worth insisting on. First, it is
told, not shown: it is the hero's account of himself. Second, its
moral logic is consistent. Odysseus loses his men in stages, and in
almost every stage the loss is caused by appetite, disobedience or his
own conspicuousness — the taunt he shouts at the blinded Cyclops gives
Polyphemus his name and makes possible the curse that follows him
home.

## Ithaca and political order

The Ithaca of the poem is not a functioning kingdom. It is a kingdom
with a vacancy. The assembly in Book 2 is the first held in twenty
years, and it achieves nothing. There is no institution capable of
removing the suitors, because the only authority the society
recognises is the absent king's, and his heir is too young to be
obeyed.

This is why the poem is a political work and not only a domestic one.
It is an anatomy of what happens to a community when legitimate
authority is absent for a generation: the young men of the leading
families consume the royal estate, courtship becomes a mechanism for
transferring power, the household staff divide, and no lawful process
exists to stop any of it. The suitors are not bandits. They are the
aristocracy of the region, and their claim — that a woman whose
husband is twenty years gone should remarry, and that whoever marries
her has a claim on the kingdom — is not absurd. That is what makes the
situation intractable.

## Odysseus as a complex hero

Odysseus is defined by *mētis*, cunning intelligence — practical,
adaptive, deceptive, opposed in Greek thought to brute force. He is
also a liar. He lies to Athena when she meets him disguised on Ithaca,
and she laughs and praises him for it. He tells at least four
elaborate false life stories in the second half of the poem. He tests
people who have every claim on his trust. He is capable of great
patience and of catastrophic self-indulgence, and the poem records
both without adjudicating.

He is also frightened, exhausted and often weeping. The poem's
Odysseus cries more than any modern adaptation has been willing to
show: on Calypso's shore, at the Phaeacian court when the bard sings
about Troy, on being recognised. His standing epithet is *polytlas*,
"much-enduring".

Reducing him to a clever action hero flattens the thing the Greeks
found most interesting about him. So does reducing him to a war-damaged
everyman. He is a king trying to get back a kingdom, and both his
virtues and his vices are the virtues and vices of that project.

See <a href="/figures/odysseus">Odysseus</a> and
<a href="/themes/cunning-and-metis">Cunning and mētis</a>.

## Penelope and intelligence

Penelope holds the position for twenty years. Her defence is the
famous trick of the shroud: she tells the suitors she will choose when
she has finished weaving a burial cloth for Laertes, and unpicks by
night what she weaves by day, for three years, until a maid betrays
her. That is not passivity. It is the same weapon her husband uses,
applied continuously under worse conditions.

The poem is unusually interested in what she knows and when. She
interviews the disguised beggar at length in Book 19, sets the contest
of the bow immediately afterwards — a contest only her husband can
win, announced the moment a stranger has told her that her husband is
near — and then, after the killing, refuses to accept the man in front
of her until she has tested him with a secret about their bed that
only he could know. Whether she has recognised him earlier is one of
the oldest interpretative questions in Homeric scholarship and it has
no agreed answer.

What is not in question is that the poem treats her intelligence as
comparable to his. When they are finally reunited the narrator
compares her to a shipwrecked swimmer sighting land — a simile that
belongs to him, transferred to her.

See <a href="/figures/penelope">Penelope</a> and

## Telemachus and maturation

The first four books are his. He begins unable to control his own
house, is prompted by Athena in disguise to act, calls an assembly
that fails, goes to sea without telling his mother, and is received by
Nestor and by Menelaus and Helen as his father's son. The journey
gives him almost no information. What it gives him is a demonstration
that other households work, and standing as a person who has been
received abroad.

By the time he returns he can hold a room. In the killing he fights
beside his father; afterwards he takes charge of the execution of the
disloyal maids, and does it with a cruelty the poem does not soften.
The maturation the poem depicts is not moral improvement. It is the
acquisition of authority, and the poem is candid about what authority
consists of.

See <a href="/figures/telemachus">Telemachus</a>.

## Hospitality and xenia

*Xenia*, guest-friendship, is the moral spine of the poem. It is not
politeness. It is a reciprocal institution with divine sanction —
Zeus is *Zeus Xeinios*, protector of guests — governing how strangers
are received, in a world with no other mechanism for safe movement
between communities. The correct sequence is fixed: receive, seat,
wash, feed, and only then ask who the guest is.

The poem tests it in every episode. The Phaeacians perform it
perfectly and convey Odysseus home. Nestor and Menelaus perform it
well. Circe and Calypso perform a distorted version, where the
hospitality becomes detention. Polyphemus inverts it completely: asked
for the guest-gift, he says his gift will be to eat Odysseus last.
And the suitors violate it from the inside, as guests consuming a
host's substance — which is why the poem regards their deaths as just.

See <a href="/themes/hospitality-and-xenia">Hospitality and xenia</a>.

## Identity, disguise, and recognition

The second half of the poem is a sequence of recognitions, each one
constructed differently, and this is where its literary sophistication
is most concentrated. Telemachus is told. Eurycleia the old nurse
discovers it by touch, finding the boar-scar on his thigh while
washing his feet, and the poem breaks off for eighty lines to tell the
story of how he got the scar before letting her drop his foot.
Eumaeus and Philoetius are shown the scar deliberately, as
credentials. The dog Argos, lying on a dung-heap, knows him
immediately, wags his tail, and dies.

Penelope's recognition is the most elaborate and the most withheld.
She orders the marriage bed moved out of the chamber; Odysseus reacts
with anger, because he built the bed himself around a living olive
tree rooted in the ground and it cannot be moved. The secret is the
immovability of the marriage, expressed as a fact about furniture.

That scene is the poem's climax in the sense that matters. The killing
resolves the political problem; the bed resolves the human one.

See <a href="/themes/identity-and-recognition">Identity and recognition</a>.

## Memory and storytelling

The *Odyssey* contains its own theory of poetry. Bards appear in it as
professionals with a social function: Phemius on Ithaca, singing under
duress to the suitors, and blind Demodocus among the Phaeacians. When
Demodocus sings about Troy, Odysseus weeps, and the poem compares him
to a woman being dragged into slavery over the body of her husband —
placing the sacker of cities, for the length of a simile, in the
position of his own victims.

The wanderings are themselves a performance: the hero singing his own
story to an audience that is deciding whether to help him. The poem
is aware that this is a reason for caution. When Odysseus finishes, his
host remarks that he is not like the liars and cheats one meets — a
compliment the audience is in a position to weigh.

See <a href="/themes/memory-and-storytelling">Memory and storytelling</a>.

## Loyalty and betrayal

The household divides. Eumaeus the swineherd, himself a slave and by
his own account a kidnapped king's son, keeps faith. So does Philoetius
the cowherd, and Eurycleia the nurse. Against them: Melanthius the
goatherd, who kicks the disguised Odysseus and later arms the suitors,
and Melantho the maid, who insults him.

The poem's interest is in loyalty maintained without evidence, over
twenty years, by people with nothing to gain. It is also unsentimental
about the reward: Eumaeus and Philoetius are promised wives, property
and standing as brothers to Telemachus — payment, publicly stated.

See <a href="/themes/loyalty-in-the-odyssey">Loyalty in the Odyssey</a>.

## Violence and restoration

Book 22 kills a hundred and eight men in a hall. It is a massacre, and
the poem describes it in detail, with the floor running and the bodies
heaped. The suitors ask for terms; Odysseus refuses them. Two men are
spared — a herald and the bard Phemius — on the grounds that they were
compelled.

Then the twelve maids who had slept with the suitors are made to carry
out the bodies and clean the hall, and are hanged. Odysseus orders a
clean death by sword; Telemachus refuses it and hangs them instead,
and the poem compares them to thrushes caught in a net, kicking a
little. It is one of the most disturbing passages in Greek literature
and it is not presented as a crime.

The violence is restorative in the poem's own logic: the suitors broke
*xenia* under Zeus's protection, and their deaths reset the order they
broke. That logic is intelligible without being ours. A serious
reading has to hold both — that the poem regards this as justice, and
that the enslaved women who are killed had no capacity to consent to
anything.

See <a href="/themes/violence-and-restoration">Violence and restoration</a>.

## The gods and human agency

The gods are characters. They meet, argue, take sides and intervene
directly. Athena is Odysseus's patron and is present throughout,
disguised as Mentes, as Mentor, as a shepherd, as a girl with a
pitcher; Poseidon pursues him for the blinding of his son Polyphemus;
Zeus arbitrates. The poem opens with Zeus complaining that mortals
blame the gods for troubles they bring on themselves — a statement of
the poem's position on responsibility, in the first divine speech.

Homeric religion is not a system of belief in the modern sense and the
gods are not moral exemplars. They are powers with interests, bound by
*themis* and by each other. Divine intervention in Homer characteristically
works through a human faculty rather than around it: Athena "puts
strength" into someone, or an idea into their mind, and the person
still acts. Double motivation of this kind is the norm, and reading it
as a denial of human agency misreads how the poem's causation works.

See <a href="/themes/divine-agency-in-homer">Divine agency in Homer</a>.

## Slavery, hierarchy, and household order

The *oikos* of the poem runs on slave labour, and the poem is entirely
open about it. Eumaeus was bought. Eurycleia was bought, by Laertes,
and the price is given. Female slaves grind grain through the night;
one of them, exhausted, prays for the suitors' last meal, and her
prayer is taken as an omen. Twelve of them are executed for sexual
relations with the suitors, relations they were in no position to
refuse.

Modern readers are sometimes told that noticing this is an imposition
of present-day values. It is not: the poem itself distinguishes
between fair and unfair treatment of slaves, and Eumaeus states that a
man loses half his worth on the day he is enslaved. The hierarchy is
described from inside, by people who accept it and who nonetheless
know what it costs.

Any account of the poem that omits this is describing a different and
more comfortable work.

## The problem of the suitors

The suitors number a hundred and eight, from Ithaca and three
neighbouring islands, with a herald, a bard and two attendants. They
are not a mob; they are the region's young aristocracy, and they have
been in the house for years.

Their offence, precisely stated, is not wanting to marry Penelope. It
is courting her in her husband's house at her husband's expense
instead of in her father's house with gifts, as custom required —
consuming the estate they hope to inherit. The poem builds a graded
case against them: Antinous is the worst, Eurymachus the most
plausible liar, Amphinomus decent enough that Odysseus warns him to
leave, which he does not. Amphinomus dies with the rest.

That gradation matters. The poem takes care to establish that they
were warned, that they were offered a way out, and that one of them
was better than the others and died anyway. It is not interested in
making the massacre easy.

## The ending and restoration of authority

The poem does not end with the reunion. A note preserved in the
scholia records that the Alexandrian scholars Aristophanes and
Aristarchus marked line 23.296 — the point at which husband and wife
go to bed — as the *end* of the poem, and the status of what follows
has been argued over ever since.

What follows is Book 24: a second scene among the dead, in which the
suitors' shades tell Agamemnon what happened and he pronounces
Penelope's fame secure; the reunion with Laertes in his orchard, where
Odysseus tests his own grieving father with another false story before
proving himself by naming the trees Laertes gave him as a boy; and
then the families of the dead suitors arming for vengeance. Three
generations of the family stand together in the field. Athena stops
the fighting, imposes an oath of peace, and the poem ends.

The ending is therefore not restoration achieved but restoration
imposed. Without divine intervention Ithaca falls into a blood feud,
because killing a hundred and eight aristocrats does not solve a
political problem, it creates one. The poem knows that. This is the
single most important thing about the ending and the thing adaptations
most often discard.

## Myth versus historical evidence

The *Odyssey* is not a historical source and does not present itself
as one. It is set in a heroic past its own audience regarded as
distant, and it contains material of visibly different periods — bronze
weapons and iron tools, cremation, Phoenician traders, assemblies that
look like later civic practice.

Whether anything corresponds to Odysseus, to his Ithaca, or to a
Trojan War is treated at length elsewhere on this platform, with each
claim carrying an explicit evidence level:
<a href="/guides/odyssey-myth-and-history">Odyssey: myth and history</a>,
<a href="/guides/was-odysseus-real">Was Odysseus real?</a>,
<a href="/guides/was-ithaca-homers-ithaca">Was Ithaca Homer's Ithaca?</a>,
and <a href="/guides/odyssey-and-mycenaean-greece">The Odyssey and Mycenaean Greece</a>.

The short version: the poem is literary, some of its furniture is old
and real, the identification of Hisarlik with Ilios is probable, the
existence of Odysseus is unknown, and no amount of excavation is going
to bear on Circe.

## Reception in antiquity

The poem was the common inheritance of Greek culture and was argued
with rather than merely admired. Plato treats Homer as the great rival
of philosophy in education and excludes most poetry from the ideal
city. Aristotle in the *Poetics* praises the *Odyssey*'s construction
and takes it as a model of unified plot.

Odysseus himself split the tradition. The Cynics and Stoics made him a
model of endurance and self-sufficiency. Attic tragedy frequently did
the opposite: the Odysseus of Sophocles' *Philoctetes* is a
manipulator, and Euripides uses him similarly. Latin literature
inherited the hostile version — for Virgil, whose Trojan perspective
requires it, Ulysses is cruel and treacherous. Dante puts him in the
eighth circle and gives him a magnificent final voyage that has no
warrant in Homer at all.

The geographers argued about his route. Strabo discusses whether the
wanderings can be located and is sceptical of literal-minded mapping,
while granting that the poet worked from real knowledge of the sea.

## Later European reception

The Latin West largely lost the Greek text and knew the Trojan story
through Latin intermediaries. Byzantium kept it; Eustathius of
Thessalonica's twelfth-century commentary is vast. Print restored it to
the West: the Greek editio princeps appeared at Florence in 1488.

In English the poem has been continuously rewritten. Chapman's verse
Odyssey appeared in 1614–15 and Pope's in 1725–26. Tennyson's *Ulysses*
(1842) is Dante's hero rather than Homer's. Joyce's *Ulysses* (1922)
transposes the structure onto a single Dublin day and made the poem's
architecture a permanent part of modern fiction. Later reworkings —
Kazantzakis, Derek Walcott's *Omeros*, Margaret Atwood's *The
Penelopiad*, Madeline Miller's *Circe* — have tended to take up the
characters the poem subordinates.

## Modern adaptations

Screen adaptation has been continuous and mostly unsuccessful at the
poem's actual difficulties, which are structural rather than
spectacular: retrospective narration, an absent protagonist, a hero
whose defining quality is deception, and an ending that refuses to
close.

The most recent is Christopher Nolan's *The Odyssey*, released 17 July
2026 — a film shot entirely on IMAX 70mm, with Matt Damon as Odysseus
and Anne Hathaway as Penelope. Our analysis treats it as both cinema
and adaptation, and sets its choices against the poem claim by claim:

## Recommended translations and editions

The choice of translation changes the poem more than most readers
expect. Some orientation:

- **Public domain, freely available.** Samuel Butler's prose (1900) is
  plain and readable and has no verse ambitions. Butcher and Lang
  (1879) is deliberately archaising and now reads as Victorian
  pastiche. Chapman (1614–15) and Pope (1725–26) are major English
  poems in their own right and are period interpretations as much as
  translations. A. T. Murray's Loeb prose (1919) faces the Greek.
- **Modern verse translations** by Lattimore, Fitzgerald, Fagles and
  Emily Wilson are in copyright and are not reproduced here. Lattimore
  is the most literal in line and word order; Fitzgerald and Fagles
  read most fluently as English poetry; Wilson's (2018) — the first
  published English translation of the poem by a woman — uses iambic
  pentameter and matches the Greek line for line, which produces a
  markedly faster, plainer poem.
- **Greek text.** The Oxford Classical Text of the *Odyssey* is the
  standard critical edition.

We quote sparingly, cite by book and line, and use public-domain
versions for any extended excerpt, naming the translator. See our
<a href="/sources">Sources</a> page.
