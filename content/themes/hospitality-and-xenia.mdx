## What xenia is

*Xenia* is usually translated guest-friendship, and it is not
politeness. It is a reciprocal institution with divine sanction,
binding on both parties and heritable between families, governing how
strangers are received in a world with no other mechanism for safe
movement between communities. Zeus in this aspect is *Zeus Xeinios*,
the protector of guests and suppliants.

The Greek word *xenos* means both guest and host, and also stranger.
That the same noun covers all three is the concept in miniature: the
relationship, not the person, is what is named.

## The sequence

The poem knows a fixed order and expects the audience to know it too:
receive the stranger, seat him, wash him, feed him, and only then ask
who he is. Asking first is a discourtesy, because it makes the welcome
conditional.

Departure has its own obligations: a guest-gift, and where possible
conveyance onward. The Phaeacians uniquely provide *pompē*, escort all
the way home, and it is what finally ends the wandering.

## The poem as a series of tests

Almost every episode is a variation on the pattern.

**Performed perfectly.** The Phaeacians, who receive an unnamed naked
stranger, feed him, entertain him, give him gifts and take him home.
Nestor and Menelaus, who feed Telemachus before asking his name.

**Performed by the poor.** Eumaeus the swineherd, a slave, who gives
what he has and states the principle explicitly: all strangers and
beggars are from Zeus, and a small gift given kindly is a gift.

**Distorted into detention.** Circe and Calypso, where hospitality
becomes captivity and the guest cannot leave.

**Inverted entirely.** Polyphemus, who asks who they are before feeding
them, cares nothing for Zeus, and announces that his guest-gift will
be to eat Nobody last.

**Violated from the inside.** The suitors, who are guests consuming
their host's substance. This is the poem's most sophisticated case,
because they have not broken in; they were admitted, and they abuse the
position.

## The political meaning

Xenia is how a world without states manages the movement of people.
The Cyclopes are described as having no assemblies and no established
rights, and it is in that context that Polyphemus eats his guests: the
absence of hospitality and the absence of institutions are the same
fact.

This is why the killing of the suitors is presented as just rather than
as vengeance. Their offence is not desiring Penelope; it is consuming a
household while enjoying its protection, and doing so against warnings.
Under the poem's terms they have attacked the only institution holding
the society together, and Zeus's protection of guests is also, in the
end, a protection of hosts.

## The ethical tension

The poem's own standard is applied unevenly, and it does not hide this.
Odysseus sacks the city of the Cicones on his way home from Troy, which
is not treated as a breach of anything. He enters the Cyclops's cave
uninvited, eats the cheese, and stays to see what gift he will be
given. Hospitality in the Odyssey is a real obligation and also a
resource the clever exploit.

## Later interpretation

The concept passed into Greek political thought as a model of
obligation to the outsider, and into Roman practice as *hospitium*. It
remains the standard example in modern classical scholarship of a moral
institution carried entirely by custom and religion rather than by law.
