## The word

*Nostos* is the return home, and it gave us *nostalgia* — literally
the pain of the return, a coinage of the seventeenth century rather
than a Greek word. In Homeric usage it names both the act of getting
back and a whole class of stories: the returns of the Achaeans from
Troy, of which the *Odyssey* is one and the murder of Agamemnon
another.

The Greek category is important because it is not sentimental. A
*nostos* is a completed journey with a successful arrival, and the
poem is full of returns that fail. The men who died at sea did not have
one; Agamemnon, who arrived and was murdered at dinner, is the poem's
standing example that arriving is not sufficient.

## What it costs

The *Odyssey* fixes the value of homecoming by making it expensive.
Calypso offers immortality and eternal youth, in a beautiful place,
without conditions. Odysseus refuses, saying he knows his wife is
lesser than a goddess to look at and that he wants his return
regardless, through further suffering if necessary.

That refusal is the poem's central assertion. Being mortal, in a
particular place, with particular people, is worth more than being a
god somewhere else. Everything that follows — twenty years, a drowned
crew, a massacre, a marriage bed rooted in the ground — is the working
out of a decision made on a beach in Book 5.

## Arrival is not restoration

The poem is careful to separate three things that adaptations tend to
collapse.

**Arrival.** Odysseus lands on Ithaca in Book 13, asleep, and does not
recognise it. Eleven books remain.

**Recognition.** He has to be identified — by a nurse, a dog, two
herdsmen, a son, and finally by his wife on her own terms in Book 23.
Getting home is not the same as being known to be home.

**Restoration.** The suitors have to be dealt with, and dealing with
them creates a blood feud that Athena has to suppress by force in the
last hundred lines. The house is recovered; the community is only
pacified.

## The unfinished return

Tiresias's prophecy in Book 11 puts a further journey after the poem
ends: Odysseus must travel inland carrying an oar until he meets people
who take it for a winnowing fan, sacrifice there to Poseidon, and come
home to a gentle death from the sea in prosperous old age.

The Odyssey therefore does not end with the homecoming complete. The
sea-god is still owed. This is the detail that makes the poem's
treatment of return serious rather than consoling, and it is almost
always cut.

## Later interpretation

The *nostos* pattern became one of the most durable structures in
European literature — Virgil inverts it into a foundation story where
the hero must not go home, and Joyce transposes it onto a single day in
Dublin. Dante and Tennyson took the opposite path and made Odysseus a
man who cannot stay, which is a reading of the further journey rather
than of the poem.
