## What happens

In Book 22 Odysseus kills a hundred and eight men in his own hall,
having barred the doors. Terms are offered and refused. Full
restitution is offered by Eurymachus and refused. Two men are spared —
the bard Phemius and the herald Medon — on the ground that they acted
under compulsion. A third, the seer Leiodes, is killed while
supplicating.

Then twelve enslaved women who had slept with the suitors are made to
carry out the bodies and wash the hall, and are hanged. Odysseus orders
a clean death by sword; Telemachus refuses it, saying they poured shame
on his mother and himself, and hangs them in a row from a ship's cable.
The poem compares them to thrushes or doves caught in a net, and says
their feet twitched a little, but not for long.

Melanthius the goatherd is mutilated and killed. The hall is purified
with fire and sulphur.

## The case the poem builds

The Odyssey does not present this as an atrocity, and it does not
present it as easy. It spends twenty-one books constructing a
justification.

The suitors violate *xenia* under Zeus's protection, consuming a
household while enjoying its shelter. They are warned repeatedly — by
Telemachus in assembly, by the seer Halitherses, by omens, by
Theoclymenus's vision of the hall running with blood, and privately by
Odysseus himself, who tells Amphinomus to go home. They plot to murder
Telemachus. They throw objects at a beggar three separate times.

The word the poem uses is *tisis*, retribution: payment exacted for an
offence. Within the poem's terms this is not vengeance but the
restoration of an order the suitors broke.

## Where the case does not hold

Three things resist the justification, and the poem includes all three.

**Amphinomus.** He is decent, he is warned, he is troubled, and he is
killed anyway — because, the poem says, Athena had bound him to his
death. The massacre is just and indiscriminate at once.

**Leiodes.** Killed in the act of supplication, which the poem
elsewhere treats as inviolable.

**The women.** Enslaved people executed for sexual relations they were
in no position to refuse. The poem gives them a simile that no reader
forgets — birds hoping for a roost and finding a snare — which is not
the language of a text that thinks nothing is happening.

## The refusal to exult

Immediately after the killing, Eurycleia begins to raise the ululation
of triumph over the dead, and Odysseus stops her: it is not *hosiē*,
not sanctioned, to glory over slain men.

He says this standing in a hall full of bodies, minutes before ordering
an execution. The poem places the two side by side without comment, and
the juxtaposition is not accidental.

## Restoration is not achieved by the violence

This is the poem's most serious statement and the one adaptations most
often cut.

Book 24 shows the consequence: the families of the dead assemble,
Eupeithes calls for vengeance, and about half the island arms. Ithaca is
one scene from civil war. Killing a hundred and eight aristocrats has
not solved a political problem; it has created a larger one.

Athena stops the fighting and imposes oaths. The peace of Ithaca is
imposed from outside, by force, in the last hundred lines. The poem
ends there because there is nothing further to say: violence recovered
the household and could not settle the community.

## Later interpretation

The hanging of the maids has become a focus of modern reception —
Margaret Atwood's *The Penelopiad* (2005) narrates the poem from their
side, and Emily Wilson's 2018 translation was widely noted for
declining the moralising nouns earlier English versions had used for
them. That attention is a response to something in the text, not an
imposition on it: the poem chose to describe them dying.
