## The poem's own statement

The Odyssey puts its position on divine responsibility in the first
speech any god makes in it. Zeus, opening the council in Book 1,
complains that mortals blame the gods for their troubles when they
bring suffering on themselves beyond their allotted share by their own
recklessness — and gives Aegisthus as the example, a man warned by the
gods who took another man's wife and life anyway.

The proem has already said the same thing about Odysseus's crew: he
could not save them, and they perished by their own *atasthaliai*,
their reckless acts, eating the cattle of the Sun.

Homeric theology is therefore not fatalism, and reading it as fatalism
misdescribes the poem's causation.

## Double motivation

The characteristic Homeric pattern is that a god acts through a human
faculty rather than around it. Athena "puts strength into" someone, or
puts a thought in their mind, or takes away their wits — and the person
then acts, and is held responsible for acting.

Nausicaa is a clear case. Athena puts courage in her when the other
girls flee, and the poem also says, separately, that she stood her
ground. Both are stated. The god is not a substitute for the decision;
the god is the decision, described from the other side.

Modern readers tend to want one explanation or the other: either she
was brave or she was divinely compelled. The poem declines the choice,
and does so consistently enough that it should be read as a considered
position rather than as confusion.

## Gods as powers, not exemplars

Athena is not good and Poseidon is not evil. They are powers with
interests, and the interest that matters most to them is *timē*, honour
as due standing.

Poseidon's complaint in Book 13 is not that a criminal escaped
punishment. It is that if he lets the Phaeacians convey Odysseus home
unchallenged he will be held cheap among the gods. The demand is for
standing, and Zeus grants it.

A reader who expects divine justice will find the poem incoherent. A
reader who expects divine interest will find it entirely consistent.

## Where the gods stop

Two limits are worth noting.

The gods are constrained by each other and by *moira*, the allotted
portion — Zeus can lament a death he cannot prevent without breaking
the order he maintains.

And divine intervention does not solve the poem's human problems. Athena
gets Odysseus home; she does not restore his household, which he does
himself with a bow. What she does at the very end is impose a peace by
force, which stops a war and settles nothing.

## The Odyssey against the Iliad

The two poems differ here. The *Iliad*'s gods are more frequently and
more violently present, and its divine scenes are often comic against a
tragic human foreground. The *Odyssey*'s divine machinery is narrower —
Athena, Poseidon, Zeus arbitrating — and more consistently moralised:
its gods talk about desert.

Some scholars take this as evidence of a later composition and of
developing religious thought. It is a reasonable inference and it is
not settled.

## The Greek terms

*Moira*, allotted portion, fate. *Timē*, honour as standing. *Themis*,
established right. *Atē*, the ruinous blindness a god may send — which
in Homer explains rather than excuses.
