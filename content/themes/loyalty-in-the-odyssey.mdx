## Loyalty without evidence

The interesting thing about loyalty in the *Odyssey* is that it is
maintained without grounds. Odysseus has been gone twenty years. Every
report of him has been false. Eumaeus says plainly that he no longer
believes travellers who bring news, having been deceived before by an
Aetolian with a good story.

He keeps faith anyway. So does Philoetius the cowherd, and Eurycleia
the nurse, and Penelope. None of them has a reason in the ordinary
sense; what they have is a relation to a household.

## The division of the house

The poem sorts its household staff carefully, and the sorting does not
follow status.

Loyal: Eumaeus, a bought slave who runs the pig farm and performs the
poem's best hospitality out of poverty. Philoetius, the cowherd.
Eurycleia, bought by Laertes and named with her price. Dolius the old
gardener and his sons.

Disloyal: Melanthius the goatherd, who kicks the disguised beggar on
the road and later arms the suitors from the storeroom. Melantho the
maid, raised by Penelope herself, who insults the beggar and sleeps
with Eurymachus. Eleven other women.

Against all of them, the suitors — free, aristocratic, and guests.

The poem's arrangement is deliberate: the people with the least reason
to be faithful are the faithful ones, and the men with the most
standing are the threat.

## What loyalty is paid

The *Odyssey* is not sentimental about this. When Odysseus reveals
himself to Eumaeus and Philoetius in Book 21, he does it with an offer:
wives, property beside his own house, and standing as companions and
brothers to Telemachus. The loyalty is rewarded, publicly, in the
currency of the society.

That is not a criticism of the poem. It is the poem being accurate
about a world where standing is the only security a dependant has.

## What disloyalty is paid

Twelve women are hanged in Book 22 for having slept with the suitors,
and Melanthius is mutilated and killed. The women were enslaved and had
no capacity to refuse anything.

The poem presents this as the just punishment of disloyalty. Reading it
that way and also seeing that it is the killing of people who could not
consent are not alternatives; they are both required, and the poem's
own simile — birds caught in a net, feet twitching a little — does not
let a reader look away.

## Penelope's case

Hers is the hardest kind. Eumaeus keeps faith with a master; Penelope
keeps faith in circumstances where faith is indistinguishable from
obstinacy, under pressure from her own father and brothers to remarry,
with no news, for twenty years, while managing a household under
occupation.

Agamemnon among the dead in Book 24 awards her the poem's last word on
fame: her *kleos* will never perish, and singers will make a song for
her. He says it in explicit contrast to his own wife.

## The Greek terms

*Pistis*, trust or good faith. *Philos*, which covers friend, kin and
one's own — the loyalty of the Odyssey runs along lines of belonging
rather than of affection. *Dmōs*, the household slave: the word for the
people the poem's loyalty mostly comes from.

## Later interpretation

The Odyssey's association of fidelity with the lowest-status characters
was noticed early and has been read in very different ways — as
aristocratic condescension, as a genuine ethical claim about worth
independent of rank, and as the household ideology of a society that
needed its dependants to be reliable. The text supports argument on all
three.
