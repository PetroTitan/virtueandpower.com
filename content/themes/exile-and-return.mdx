## A poem full of displaced people

Odysseus is only the most conspicuous. The *Odyssey* is populated by
people who are not where they came from, and it notices all of them.

Eumaeus was stolen as a child from an island where his father was king
and sold into slavery. The Phoenician woman who took him had herself
been kidnapped by pirates. Eurycleia was bought. The twelve women in
the household were acquired somehow, and the poem does not say how.
Theoclymenus is a fugitive seer who has killed a man and needs
passage. The suitors' families face the choice at the end between
staying and feud.

And Odysseus's false life stories — the Cretan tales he tells four
times — are all stories of displacement: a bastard son, a raider, a
captive in Egypt, a man passed from hand to hand. He invents, over and
over, a life like the ones around him.

## Displacement as the ordinary condition

The poem's world assumes movement, and mostly involuntary movement.
Piracy is a recognised activity — Nestor asks Telemachus, politely,
whether he is a trader or a pirate, and it is not an insult. Slavery
supplies households. Ships are lost. The sea is the medium of exchange
and the mechanism of ruin.

Against that background the *oikos* is not a sentimental attachment. It
is the only thing that makes a person more than a body available for
sale, which is why Eumaeus can say that Zeus takes away half a man's
worth on the day he becomes a slave, and why getting home is worth
refusing immortality for.

## What return does not undo

The poem is precise that coming back does not reverse having been away.

Odysseus arrives with nothing left of what he took: twelve ships gone,
every companion dead. He arrives asleep and does not recognise the
place. He then spends eleven books unable to be known, living in his
own house as a beggar, being kicked and struck by men eating his
property.

When he is finally recognised, he tells his wife that there is more
travelling to come — inland, with an oar, until he meets people who do
not know the sea.

The homecoming is real and it is not a restoration of the status quo
ante. Twenty years happened.

## The exile who cannot return

The poem's counter-figures are the ones for whom return is closed.
Agamemnon returned and was murdered. Achilles is dead and says he would
rather be a landless labourer among the living. Elpenor died on Circe's
roof and asks only for a grave with an oar planted on it, so that
someone will know he was there.

Elpenor is the poem's smallest and clearest statement on the subject: a
man with no achievements at all, whose entire request is not to be
unmarked.

## The Greek terms

*Nostos*, the return; *nostimon ēmar*, the day of homecoming, which
characters speak of as a thing that can be taken away from them.
*Xenos*, the stranger — and the guest, and the host. *Phugē*, flight.

## Later interpretation

The Odyssey has been read persistently in the twentieth and
twenty-first centuries as a poem about veterans and about refugees, and
both readings find real purchase: the poem is unusually attentive to
what displacement does to people, and unusually unwilling to promise
that arrival repairs it. The risk in such readings is anachronism —
the poem has no concept of trauma and no concept of the refugee as a
legal category — and the discipline is to say what the text does and
does not contain.
