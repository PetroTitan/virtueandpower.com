## An unusual range

Compared with the *Iliad*, the *Odyssey* is full of women, and they do
a great deal. Penelope governs a household under siege for twenty
years. Circe and Calypso are powers who detain the hero. Nausicaa makes
the judgement that saves his life. Arete is supplicated in preference
to her husband. Helen drugs the wine and tells her own version of
Troy. Athena runs the plot. Eurycleia recognises him first. Anticleia
explains the nature of death.

This is not a modern discovery. The poem's ancient readers noticed it,
and Samuel Butler was moved by it to argue — with essentially no
scholarly support, but not from nowhere — that the poem was written by
a woman.

## Penelope

The fullest characterisation. Her weapon is the same as her husband's:
the shroud is *mētis*, using an obligation the suitors cannot refuse as
a mechanism of delay. She extracts gifts from the men consuming her
house. She sets the contest of the bow. And she tests the man who has
just killed a hundred and eight people, refusing to accept him until he
gives her a secret only two people know.

The test of the bed is the only occasion in the poem where anyone
outmanoeuvres Odysseus.

Whether she recognises him before Book 23 is unresolved and probably
unresolvable. See <a href="/figures/penelope">Penelope</a>.

## The powers

Circe and Calypso are both goddesses who detain him, and the poem's
treatment of both is genuinely double. Calypso keeps him seven years
while he weeps on the shore daily, and they sleep together in a formula
that records no reluctance. Circe transforms his men into animals, and
then, after an oath, becomes the most useful host in the poem.

Neither is a temptress in the later allegorical sense and neither is a
victim in the modern sense. The poem holds captivity and intimacy
together without resolving them, and modern retellings almost always
resolve them one way or the other.

## Nausicaa and Arete

Both exercise judgement that the plot depends on. Nausicaa decides,
correctly, that a large naked stranger is a suppliant rather than a
threat, and manages her own reputation while helping him. Arete is the
person whose knees Odysseus is told to grasp, and her prominence has
been discussed since antiquity as anomalous in Homer.

## The limits

The poem's range should not be overstated, and its own text prevents
overstating it.

In Book 1 Telemachus publicly orders his mother out of the hall and
tells her that speech is the business of men. The Odyssey's most
capable woman is sent upstairs by her adolescent son, and the poem
records it as his first act of authority.

Twelve enslaved women are hanged in Book 22 for sexual relations with
the suitors — relations they had no power to refuse. They are given a
simile and no names except Melantho's.

Female slaves grind grain through the night; one of them, the weakest
and last still working, prays for the suitors' last meal, and her
prayer is taken as an omen. The poem notices her, and does not free
her.

## The Greek terms

*Homophrosynē*, like-mindedness between husband and wife, named in
Book 6 as the greatest good there is. *Kleos*, fame — awarded to
Penelope explicitly in Book 24, which is the poem's last statement on
reputation and is about a woman. *Dmōai*, the enslaved women of the
household.

## Later interpretation

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century reception has concentrated on the
figures the poem subordinates: Atwood's *The Penelopiad* gives the
hanged women a chorus; Madeline Miller's *Circe* narrates from Aeaea.
Emily Wilson's 2018 translation was widely discussed for its handling
of the vocabulary applied to enslaved women. These are arguments with
the poem conducted on the poem's own material.
