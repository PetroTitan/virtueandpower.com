## The oikos

The *oikos* is the household — the family, the land, the livestock, the
stores, the slaves, and the standing that goes with them. It is the
basic unit of the world the Odyssey depicts, and it is an economic,
political and religious entity at once.

Greek political thought never quite escaped this. Aristotle's
*Politics* begins with the household and builds the city out of it, and
the word *oikonomia* — household management — is the ancestor of
economics.

The Odyssey is a poem about an *oikos* under threat, which is why a
story about a marriage is simultaneously a story about a kingdom.

## What is actually happening on Ithaca

The situation is precise and worth stating precisely, because the poem
depends on it.

Odysseus has been gone twenty years and is presumed dead by most
people. His wife has not remarried. His son is only now old enough to
act, and has no independent authority. A hundred and eight young men
of the leading families of Ithaca and three neighbouring islands are
living in the house, consuming its stores and livestock, waiting for
Penelope to choose.

Their offence is not wanting to marry her. It is the manner: courtship
should take place at the bride's father's house, with gifts given, not
at the absent husband's expense. Telemachus says this in the assembly
in Book 2 and Penelope says it to the suitors' faces in Book 18.

## The failure of institutions

Book 2 is the poem's political centre. Telemachus calls an assembly —
the first in twenty years — and it fails. The institutions exist: there
is an assembly, there are elders, there is a seer who reads the omen
correctly. They are simply unable to act, because the authority that
would enforce a decision is the king's, and the king is not there.

This is the poem's diagnosis. Ithaca is not lawless. It is a place
where law has no executive.

Everything that follows is a consequence. If no institution can remove
the suitors, the only remaining instrument is the returning king, in
person, with a bow.

## The household as a political map

The poem organises its cast by their relation to the *oikos*, and the
loyalties do not follow status.

Eumaeus and Philoetius, both slaves, keep faith. Eurycleia the nurse
keeps faith. Melanthius the goatherd and Melantho the maid do not. The
suitors, who are aristocrats and guests, are the principal threat.
Loyalty in this poem is not a function of rank, and the poem is
interested in that.

## What restoration costs

Book 22 solves the problem by killing everyone. Book 24 shows the bill:
the families of a hundred and eight dead aristocrats arm themselves,
and Ithaca is one scene away from a civil war that would destroy the
household the killing was meant to save.

Athena stops it. The peace at the end of the Odyssey is imposed by a
god, not achieved by the king.

That is the poem's final political statement, and it is not
comfortable: violence can recover a house and cannot by itself restore
a community. It is also the part of the poem adaptations most often
remove.

## Later interpretation

Moses Finley's *The World of Odysseus* (1954) read the poems as
evidence for a society organised around the household, gift-exchange
and personal followings rather than around the state, and argued it
belonged to the Early Iron Age rather than the Bronze Age. The dating
remains disputed; the structural description has been enormously
influential.
