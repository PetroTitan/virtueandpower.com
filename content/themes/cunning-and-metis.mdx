## The word

*Mētis* is practical intelligence: the capacity to find a way through a
situation that force cannot solve. It covers cunning, resourcefulness,
timing, disguise, calculation and deceit, and Greek thought treats it
as a single faculty rather than as a set of separate virtues and
vices.

It is opposed to *biē*, force. The Iliadic hero has *biē*; Odysseus has
*mētis*; the poems know the contrast and use it. Achilles hates the man
who says one thing and hides another in his heart. Odysseus does it
constantly.

Metis is also a person: in Hesiod, a Titaness, the first wife of Zeus,
whom he swallows while pregnant so that no child of hers may overthrow
him — and Athena is the child, born from his head. The goddess of
cunning intelligence is cunning intelligence, absorbed and made safe.

## Mētis in the Odyssey

The poem's fullest demonstration is the Cyclops. Trapped behind a stone
no man can move, Odysseus cannot fight his way out — killing Polyphemus
would seal them in. He therefore solves a physical problem with a
linguistic one: he gives his name as *Outis*, Nobody, so that the
victim's cry for help defeats itself.

The Greek doubles the point in a way no translation carries. *Outis*
means nobody; *mē tis*, the other way of saying no one, is a homophone
of *mētis*. Odysseus escapes by being nobody and by being cunning, in
the same sound.

Elsewhere: the wooden horse, recalled twice in the poem; the false
Cretan life stories told to Athena, Eumaeus, Penelope and Laertes; the
contest of the bow, which is arranged by his wife and which is the last
and best trick in the poem.

## Penelope has it too

The shroud is *mētis*. Penelope takes an obligation the suitors cannot
refuse — the duty owed to the dead — and turns it into three years of
delay. The test of the bed is *mētis*: she provokes her husband into
revealing himself by insulting his workmanship, and it is the only time
in the poem anyone outmanoeuvres him.

The poem's word for what makes their marriage good is *homophrosynē*,
like-mindedness. It is not affection. It is two people with the same
faculty recognising each other.

## Why it is morally unstable

Mētis has no fixed moral sign, and the Greeks did not pretend
otherwise. The same faculty that saves the crew from the cave is the
faculty that lies to a grieving father in an orchard for no reason.

The tradition split accordingly. The Cynics and Stoics made Odysseus a
model of rational self-command. Attic tragedy made him a manipulator —
Sophocles' *Philoctetes* has him arguing for deceit against a young
man's scruples. Virgil, writing from the Trojan side, made him a
deviser of crimes. Dante put him among the counsellors of fraud.

None of these is a misreading. They are different weightings of a
faculty the poem itself presents without a verdict.

## Later interpretation

The modern scholarly treatment that made *mētis* a category in its own
right is Marcel Detienne and Jean-Pierre Vernant's *Les ruses de
l'intelligence: la mētis des Grecs* (1974), which traced it across
Greek thought from Homer to the sophists. It remains the standard
starting point.
