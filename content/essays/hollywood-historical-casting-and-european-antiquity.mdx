## The complaint, stated properly

There is a complaint about how large studios handle culturally specific
ancient material, and it is worth stating in its strongest form before
testing it, because it is usually encountered in a form so weak that
dismissing it is trivial.

The strong version is this. A studio acquires a story whose value lies
in its recognition — the name *Odyssey*, the name *Achilles*, the name
*Cleopatra* — and then treats the cultural framework that produced the
story as removable packaging. What is retained is the intellectual
property: the title, the famous incidents, the recognisable silhouette.
What is discarded is everything that made those things mean what they
meant. The religion becomes decoration. The social institutions become
backdrop. The ethical framework is replaced by a contemporary one, on
the assumption that the original would not be intelligible or would not
be liked.

The result is a production that carries a culture's name and does not
carry the culture. And because the name is what the audience recognises,
what most people will thereafter believe about the *Odyssey* is what the
film showed them.

That is a real argument about cultural transmission, and it does not
require any claim about anyone's ancestry to make.

## Where the complaint has purchase

Several patterns are visible enough to describe without conspiracy.

**Famous names retained, frameworks discarded.** This is the central
one. Ancient Greek religion is not decoration; it is an account of
causation in which gods act through human faculties, and characters are
responsible for actions gods prompt. A production that keeps Athena as a
figure and removes that structure has kept a name.

**Contemporary ethics substituted silently.** The *Odyssey* contains a
household running on slave labour, twelve enslaved women executed for
relations they could not refuse, and a hero who sleeps with two goddesses
while weeping for his wife. A production may decline all of that. What
it should not do is decline it and present the result as the poem, since
the audience has no way to tell.

**Revision marketed as recovery.** The most legitimate form of this
complaint. There is a real difference between "we have reinterpreted
this" and "this is what it was really like", and productions have a
commercial incentive to blur it. Reinterpretation is honourable;
reinterpretation described as restoration is not.

**Inconsistency across civilisations.** Studios that treat European
antiquity as freely reconfigurable are sometimes noticeably more careful
with the cultural specificity of other traditions. It is fair to notice
this and to ask that one standard be applied. It is not fair to infer
from it a coordinated programme, and this essay does not.

## The counterarguments, which are strong

An honest version of this argument has to concede that most of the
obvious rebuttals are correct.

**Non-literal casting is ancient theatrical practice.** Greek theatre
was performed by a small number of masked male performers doubling
multiple roles including all the women. The idea that a performer must
resemble a character is a convention of screen realism, not a principle
of dramatic art, and it is younger than most of the traditions it is
invoked to protect.

**Homeric epic is myth, not documentary.** The *Odyssey* is set in the
age of heroes — a real past in the Greek imagination, populated by
beings greater than present people, at a distance that made verification
neither possible nor interesting. Demanding documentary fidelity from it
is a category error. See
<a href="/guides/odyssey-myth-and-history">Odyssey: myth and history</a>.

**Modern racial taxonomies do not map onto antiquity.** Greek identity
was organised around language, cult, custom and political form. The word
*barbaros* is about speech. Retrojecting nineteenth- and
twentieth-century racial categories onto the Homeric world produces
claims the sources do not contain, in either direction.

**Homer barely describes anyone.** This is the fact that collapses most
appearance-based casting arguments. Helen's beauty in the *Iliad* is an
effect on old men, not a description. Penelope is characterised as
circumspect. The one substantial physical description of Odysseus occurs
at moments of divine transformation, uses contested colour vocabulary,
and has been rendered in radically different ways by serious
translators.

**Adaptation has always transformed.** The tradition did nothing else.
The wooden horse most readers picture is Virgil's, seven centuries after
Homer and from the Trojan side. Dante gave Odysseus a final voyage with
no warrant in the poem, and Tennyson made it admirable. A standard that
condemns modern reworking condemns the tradition that produced the
text.

**Strict appearance-matching oversimplifies antiquity.** The
Mediterranean of the epics is mobile and connected — Phoenician traders
in the *Odyssey* as a routine presence, Menelaus wandering to Egypt, the
Ethiopians at the ends of the earth. A casting policy built on a
homogeneous ancient world would misrepresent it.

## What survives the counterarguments

Not much of the appearance-based case, and quite a lot of the
cultural-framework case.

If Homer does not describe his characters, then a film cannot contradict
descriptions that do not exist, and criticism claiming otherwise is
asserting a source it has not read. That branch of the argument does not
survive.

What survives is more specific and more demanding. A production can be
asked whether it has kept the things that make the work that work: the
religious structure, the social institutions, the moral framework, the
particular ethical problem the text was posing. Those are checkable, and
this platform checks them — a change of Eumaeus from slave to tutor is a
verifiable alteration of a social fact the poem uses; the removal of the
test of the marriage bed is a verifiable removal of the scene that
establishes Penelope's intelligence; the framing of the Achaeans as the
Sea Peoples is a verifiable historical claim that specialists have not
established.

There is also a cultural-location argument that survives intact and is
different in kind: when a production adapts the foundational work of a
living culture and films in that country, the consistent absence of that
culture's performers from its principal cast is a discretionary choice
the production can be asked to explain. That objection was raised in
Greece about the 2026 film. It implies nothing about anyone's ancestry
or capability, and it has not been answered.

## A working vocabulary

Most of the heat in this argument comes from six different things being
called by one name. They are worth separating, because a production may
legitimately do the first four and should be criticised for the last
two.

**Adaptation** — moving a work between media, with the compressions and
transpositions that requires. Cutting the Phaeacians from a
near-three-hour film is adaptation.

**Interpretation** — resolving what the source leaves open. The
*Odyssey* never says why Circe transforms men; supplying a reason is
interpretation, and filling a silence is not contradicting a statement.

**Modernisation** — rendering an ancient framework in contemporary
terms so that it can be understood. Translating *xenia* as the law of
Zeus is modernisation done well.

**Historical reconstruction** — attempting to depict a period as
evidence suggests it was. Almost no screen antiquity attempts this, and
for Homer it is not even coherent, since the poem is not accurate to any
single period.

**Revisionism** — deliberately reworking a source to say something it
did not say. Entirely legitimate as art. It becomes a problem only when
undeclared, because the audience then takes the revision for the
original.

**Misrepresentation** — presenting a reworking as a recovery. Asserting
that this is what the poem said, or what the period was, when it is
neither.

The line this essay draws is at the boundary between the fifth and the
sixth, and it is a line about candour rather than about content. A
production is entitled to revise. It is not entitled to revise and call
it restoration.

## What follows

Three things, and they are all obligations on critics rather than on
film-makers.

Criticism should be specific. "They changed it" is not a criticism;
"they removed the scene in *Odyssey* 23 in which Penelope proves her
husband's identity by a secret about the marriage bed, which is the only
occasion in the poem when anyone outwits Odysseus" is one, and it can be
checked and answered.

Criticism should be consistent. A standard applied to one civilisation's
material and not another's is not a standard.

And criticism should decline the vocabulary that has attached itself to
this subject. Language about replacement, invasion, contamination or
corruption is not a strong version of the cultural argument; it is a
substitute for it, and it forfeits the ground the argument actually
holds. The case that studios treat European antiquity as generic
intellectual property is a case about cultural transmission, and it is
weakened, not strengthened, by being conducted as a grievance about
which people appear on screen.

The strongest form of this argument has never needed to mention
anybody's ancestry. It only needs to ask what the film kept.
