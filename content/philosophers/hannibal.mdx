## The evidential problem, first

Carthage left no historical literature that survives. Everything we have
about Hannibal comes from Greek and Roman writers — Polybius, who had
access to people who had fought against him and to a Carthaginian
inscription Hannibal himself set up, and Livy, who is later and more
rhetorical.

That means the most formidable enemy Rome faced is known through Roman
memory. Polybius is careful and comparatively fair; the tradition around
him nonetheless supplies a childhood oath of eternal enmity, a
characterisation of Punic treachery, and a moral frame in which Roman
virtue eventually prevails.

## The Italian campaign

In 218 BCE Hannibal marched from Spain across the Alps into Italy with an
army including war elephants, a crossing whose route has been argued over
for two centuries without resolution.

In three years he destroyed three Roman armies. At the Trebia he used the
cold and a concealed force to break a Roman crossing. At Lake Trasimene he
executed the largest successful ambush in ancient warfare, catching a
consular army in march column against a lake shore. At
<a href="/warfare/battles/cannae">Cannae</a> in 216 BCE he enveloped and
destroyed the largest army Rome had ever assembled.

Then the campaign stalled. Rome refused to negotiate, avoided battle under
the Fabian strategy, and rebuilt. Hannibal held the field in Italy for
fifteen years and could not take Rome or break the Italian confederation
apart, and the war was decided in Spain and Africa.

## Zama and after

Recalled to defend Carthage, he was defeated by Scipio at
<a href="/warfare/battles/zama">Zama</a> in 202 BCE — a battle in which the
cavalry superiority that had made Cannae possible was on the Roman side,
because Scipio had secured the Numidians by diplomacy first.

He survived the defeat, held political office at Carthage and reformed its
government, and was driven into exile by opponents at home and pressure
from Rome. He served at the Seleucid court and later in Bithynia, and took
poison to avoid being handed over.

## Assessment

The tactical judgement is not seriously disputed: Cannae is among the most
complete battlefield victories in recorded history, and the ambush at
Trasimene is unmatched in scale.

The strategic judgement is argued. He won every major engagement and lost
the war, and the reasons — Rome's manpower, the loyalty of most Italian
allies, Carthaginian political support that never matched the commitment,
and his own lack of a siege capability adequate to take Rome — are all
defended. What is not in doubt is that his opponents learned from him:
Scipio's tactics at Zama are Hannibal's, used against him.
