## What is attributed to him

Ancient sources name Iktinos as an architect of the Parthenon, working
with Kallikrates, and Plutarch's life of Pericles lists him among those
directing the building programme. He is also credited with the temple of
Apollo at Bassae and with work at Eleusis, and is said to have written a
treatise on the Parthenon with Karpion. The treatise is lost.

Every one of those attributions comes from later literary report. No
inscription names him on the building, and the Parthenon's own building
accounts, which survive in part and record payments in detail, do not
supply an architect's name in the way a modern reader would expect.

## What that means

The Parthenon is one of the best-documented ancient constructions and its
architect is one of the least documented ancient people. That gap is
characteristic rather than exceptional: Greek building accounts record
tasks and payments rather than authorship, because the categories the
inscriptions use are not the categories a modern architectural history
wants.

The division of responsibility between Iktinos, Kallikrates and Phidias,
who directed the sculpture, is not recoverable from the evidence.

## Bassae

The temple of Apollo Epikourios at Bassae in Arcadia, attributed to him
by Pausanias, is a strange and interesting building — unusual in
orientation, mixing the orders, and containing what may be the earliest
Corinthian capital known. Whether the attribution is correct is not
established.

## Why the platform records him

Because he is the standard case of what ancient architectural authorship
looks like: a name transmitted by literature, attached to buildings that
carry no signature, in a tradition that did not record designers the way
it recorded dedicators.
