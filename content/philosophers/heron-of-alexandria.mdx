## The works

Heron's surviving treatises are practical: the *Pneumatica* on devices
worked by air, water and steam; the *Automata* on self-moving theatrical
machinery; the *Dioptra* on a surveying instrument and its use; the
*Belopoeica* on artillery construction; and the *Metrica* on measurement,
which includes the formula for the area of a triangle from its three
sides that still carries his name.

Together they are the fullest picture we have of Alexandrian applied
mechanics, and they matter to this platform mainly for artillery and
surveying, where they transmit the calibration rules that Hellenistic
engineers actually used.

## The aeolipile

The *Pneumatica* describes a sphere made to spin by steam escaping from
bent nozzles. It is regularly presented as a steam engine that antiquity
failed to exploit.

That framing is anachronistic. The device produces rotary motion with no
mechanism for taking work off it, no boiler pressure worth the name, and
no metallurgy behind it. It sits in a book of curiosities and temple
effects alongside self-opening doors and singing birds, which is where it
belongs. The interesting question is not why nobody built a locomotive
but why a culture with this much mechanical skill directed it at wonder
and theatre.

## Dating

Heron's dates were long uncertain and are now generally fixed to the
first century CE by his own reference to a lunar eclipse that can be
identified astronomically. That is an unusually secure date for an
ancient technical author.
