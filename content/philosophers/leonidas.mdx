## What is known

Leonidas was an Agiad king of Sparta who commanded the allied Greek force
that held the pass at Thermopylae in 480 BCE and was killed there. That
sentence contains most of what can be stated without qualification.

He came to the throne some years before the invasion, following the death
of his half-brother Cleomenes I. Herodotus, our only substantial source,
gives him a genealogy running back to Heracles — a Spartan royal
convention rather than a historical record — and reports that he had not
expected to rule.

His command at Thermopylae was of an allied force of roughly 5,200 men
drawn from across the Peloponnese and central Greece, of whom 300 were
Spartiate citizens. The reduction of that force to "the 300" in popular
memory is a distortion the numbers in Herodotus do not support.

## Thermopylae

Herodotus reports that Leonidas took only men who had living sons, which
if true indicates that the assignment was understood to be dangerous from
the outset. He also reports an oracle that Sparta must either lose a king
or see the city destroyed, and presents Leonidas as acting with that in
mind.

Whether the stand was a planned sacrifice or a rearguard covering a
withdrawal cannot be determined. The two readings are both defensible from
the same text, and this platform does not choose between them. See
<a href="/warfare/battles/thermopylae">Thermopylae</a>.

His body was recovered by the Persians and, Herodotus says, treated with a
dishonour he regards as out of character for Xerxes. The bones were later
brought back to Sparta.

## What the sources are

Almost everything comes from Herodotus, writing a generation and a half
later, drawing on Spartan and other informants with reasons of their own.
Sparta produced no historical literature. Later accounts — Diodorus,
Plutarch — are further away and preserve traditions that cannot be
independently checked, including most of the famous sayings attributed to
him.

The laconic replies for which he is remembered belong to a collected
tradition of Spartan sayings assembled centuries afterwards. They are
evidence for how Greeks and Romans wanted Spartans to have spoken.

## Reception

No ancient commander has been recruited more often or by more incompatible
causes. Leonidas has been made a symbol by nineteenth-century
nationalisms, by both sides in the Second World War, and by modern
political movements across the spectrum.

This platform records that history as reception. It does not treat the
Spartan state as an object of admiration: its military capacity rested on
the permanent subjection of the helots, a population held by force and
against whom war was formally declared each year. See
<a href="/warfare/spartan-army">the Spartan army</a>.
