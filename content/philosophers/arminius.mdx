## A Roman officer

Arminius was the son of a Cheruscan chieftain, sent to Rome as a hostage or
for education, granted Roman citizenship and equestrian rank, and given
command of Cheruscan auxiliaries in Roman service. He knew the Roman army
from inside it.

That is the fact that explains
<a href="/warfare/battles/teutoburg-forest">the Teutoburg Forest</a>. The
ambush of 9 CE worked because its author understood Roman march discipline,
baggage arrangements, route selection and the assumptions a governor would
make about a friendly auxiliary commander's intelligence report.

## The destruction of Varus's army

In 9 CE Arminius reported a rising in a distant district to the governor
Publius Quinctilius Varus and offered to guide the army there. Varus moved
three legions with baggage and dependants off the prepared routes into
broken, wooded and waterlogged country.

The attack came in stages over several days on ground where the Romans
could not form a line or fortify properly. Legions XVII, XVIII and XIX were
destroyed and their numbers were never reused — an administrative silence
that is itself evidence.

## Afterwards

Germanicus campaigned east of the Rhine between 14 and 16 CE, recovered two
of the three lost eagles, visited the battlefield and buried the remains,
and fought Arminius at Idistaviso. Rome nonetheless abandoned the project of
a province beyond the Rhine.

Arminius's later career is known only in outline: conflict with his
father-in-law Segestes, who had warned Varus against him and who
surrendered Arminius's pregnant wife Thusnelda to the Romans; war with
Maroboduus of the Marcomanni; and death at the hands of his own kinsmen
around 21 CE, on Tacitus's account because he was suspected of aiming at
kingship.

Tacitus gives him an obituary calling him the liberator of Germany, and
notes that he was still sung of among the tribes but was unknown to Greek
historians and inadequately treated by Roman ones.

## Reception

From the sixteenth century Arminius was adopted as a German national
symbol under the name Hermann, and a colossal monument, the
Hermannsdenkmal, was erected in the nineteenth century in the context of
German unification. The figure was subsequently used by German nationalist
and later Nazi propaganda.

That history is a fact about modern Germany, not about 9 CE, and this
platform describes it as reception. Nothing in the ancient evidence
supports reading Arminius as a national founder: he led a coalition of
tribes for a limited period and was killed by his own people.
