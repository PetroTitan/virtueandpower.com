## A figure with one source

Everything known about Vercingetorix comes from Caesar's *Gallic War*, with
brief later notices in authors dependent on it. He is the best-attested
Gallic individual of the period and he is attested by the man who destroyed
him, in a work written to justify that destruction to a Roman readership.

Caesar's portrait is not hostile, and the reason is worth noting: a
formidable opponent makes a more impressive victory. Readers should weigh
the flattery as carefully as they would weigh abuse.

## The revolt

In 52 BCE, with Caesar wintering in Italy, a coordinated rising broke out
across Gaul. Vercingetorix, an Arvernian noble whose father had been killed
for seeking kingship, took the leadership.

His strategy was the significant departure from previous Gallic resistance.
Rather than seeking pitched battle against legions, he adopted attrition:
denying supplies, burning settlements that could feed the Romans, avoiding
engagement, and striking at foraging parties and communications. Caesar
describes the policy in detail, including the decision to spare the
oppidum of Avaricum against Vercingetorix's advice — which Caesar then took,
with the slaughter of its population.

At Gergovia the Romans were repulsed, the only clear defeat Caesar
acknowledges in the campaign.

## Alesia

The strategy failed when Vercingetorix allowed himself to be shut up in
the hill fort of <a href="/warfare/battles/alesia">Alesia</a>. Caesar built
two concentric lines of fortification — inward to contain, outward to hold
off the relief army — and won the resulting battle on ground his legions
had constructed.

Vercingetorix surrendered the next day. Caesar's account of the surrender is
brief and matter-of-fact; the dramatic scene of a warrior riding out to
throw down his arms at Caesar's feet is Plutarch's elaboration, written
much later.

He was held for six years, displayed in Caesar's triumph of 46 BCE, and
executed afterwards, which was standard Roman treatment of a captured enemy
leader.

## Reception

From the nineteenth century Vercingetorix became a French national symbol,
promoted particularly under Napoleon III, who funded the excavations at
Alise-Sainte-Reine and erected a monumental statue there. That afterlife is
a fact about modern France and is recorded here as reception rather than as
evidence about the first century BCE.
