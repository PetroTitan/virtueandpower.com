## A brief orientation

"Homer" is the name the Greeks gave to the author of the *Iliad* and
the *Odyssey*. Whether a single historical poet by that name composed
both works, whether the two were composed by different poets, and
whether the texts as we have them stand at the end of a long oral
tradition of bardic performance — these are the questions of what
modern scholarship calls the Homeric Question, and they are open.

## What is known, and what is not

What can be stated without qualification is narrow. Two long poems in
dactylic hexameter survive, divided in transmission into twenty-four
books each. They are the earliest substantial European literature we
have. From at least the sixth century BCE the Greeks treated them as
the work of one poet named Homer, and treated that poet as the head of
their literature.

Everything past that is inference. The conventional eighth-century
dating is a modern reconstruction from language, material culture and
historical reference, not a transmitted fact. Herodotus, our most
useful ancient witness, offers his own estimate — that Hesiod and Homer
lived some four hundred years before him and no more (*Histories* 2.53)
— and presents it as an argument against people who thought them older.

The ancient biographical tradition is abundant and untrustworthy.
Several cities claimed him; the lists of claimants do not agree. The
tradition of his blindness draws strength from the blind Phaeacian bard
Demodocus in *Odyssey* 8, who is a character in a poem rather than a
self-portrait. The surviving *Lives* are late compilations. Whether
there was a poet named Homer at all is not something the evidence
settles, and this platform does not pretend that it does.

## The two poems

The *Iliad* takes a few weeks of the tenth and final year of the Greek
war against Troy and makes its subject the wrath — *mēnis* — of
Achilles, in around 15,700 lines. It ends with Priam ransoming
Hector's body. The fall of Troy is not in it.

The *Odyssey* tells the homecoming — *nostos* — of Odysseus and the
restoration of his household on Ithaca, in around 12,100 lines. It is
the later poem on most current views, and it is a different kind of
work: domestic where the *Iliad* is martial, interested in disguise,
recognition, endurance and the government of a household, and
structured around a hero who does not appear until its fifth book and
who narrates his own adventures in retrospect.

Both were the foundation of Greek *paideia* for as long as there was a
Greek *paideia* to speak of.

in the background. Plato's *Republic* spends considerable space arguing
about the place of Homer in education, and the seriousness of the worry

him in the *Poetics* as the model of epic construction and quotes him
