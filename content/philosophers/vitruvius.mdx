## Why he matters disproportionately

Marcus Vitruvius Pollio is not the greatest architect of antiquity and
may not have been a distinguished one at all. He matters because his
book survived and everyone else's did not.

*De architectura*, in ten books, is the only substantial architectural
treatise to come down from the ancient world. It covers town planning,
building materials, temples and the orders, public buildings, private
houses, water supply, geometry and astronomy as an architect needs them,
and machines. Almost everything modern scholarship says about ancient
architectural theory begins with it, and that dependence is itself a
problem: a single voice has become the whole ancient position.

## The man

Little is established. He served in some engineering capacity under
Caesar, and he addresses the work to Augustus, whose sister he says
supported him. He mentions one building of his own, a basilica at Fano,
which does not survive.

He writes as a practitioner reaching the end of a career, anxious that
his profession is undervalued, and inclined to explain forms by telling
stories about their origins.

## What is in the book, and how to read it

The famous elements — firmness, utility and delight as the qualities of
good building; the orders and their proportions; the anthropomorphic
origin stories for Doric, Ionic and Corinthian; the account of pozzolana
and of why lead pipes are objectionable — are all genuinely useful and
none should be taken as reportage.

The origin stories in particular are aetiology. The orders predate him by
centuries, and no earlier source explains them as he does. His
proportional schemes are also more regular than the buildings: measured
surveys of surviving temples show variation the canon conceals.

## Afterlife

The treatise was known through the Middle Ages and became prescriptive
in the Renaissance, when Alberti, Palladio and others took it as the
authority on classical building. Through them the Vitruvian vocabulary
reached almost every public building in Europe and its colonies. The
drawing known as Vitruvian Man is Leonardo's illustration of a passage in
Book 3 on human proportion.

That afterlife is why a Greek temple's vocabulary is legible on a
nineteenth-century bank, and why this platform treats Vitruvius as the
hinge between ancient practice and later European building.
