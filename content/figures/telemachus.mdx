## In the Odyssey

He was an infant when his father sailed. He has no memory of him, and
says so: he knows his father only by report, and remarks that no man
really knows his own begetting.

The first four books are his. He begins unable to control his own
house — the suitors laugh at him — and is prompted by Athena, disguised
as a family guest-friend, to act. He calls an assembly, the first in
twenty years, and it achieves nothing. He then takes a ship without
telling his mother and goes to Pylos and to Sparta.

The journey gives him almost no information about his father. What it
gives him is different and more useful: he is received as a guest of
standing in two functioning households, seen how they work, and been
treated by Nestor, Menelaus and Helen as his father's son. He comes
back able to hold a room.

In Book 16 his father reveals himself with no proof at all, and
Telemachus initially refuses to believe it. Thereafter he keeps the
secret under sustained provocation, publicly rebukes suitors who abuse
the beggar, removes the weapons from the hall, and fights beside his
father in Book 22.

## The maturation, and what kind it is

It is tempting to read the Telemachy as a coming-of-age in the modern
sense, in which a young person becomes wiser and better. That is not
what the poem shows.

What Telemachus acquires is authority. He learns to speak in assembly,
to command servants, to be obeyed by his mother — and the first thing
he does with it, in Book 1, is publicly order Penelope out of the hall
and tell her that speech is the business of men.

And in Book 22 he takes charge of the execution of the twelve enslaved
women. His father orders a clean death by sword; Telemachus refuses it,
saying they poured shame on his mother and on him, and hangs them
instead. The poem compares them to birds caught in a net. This is the
end point of his development, and any account of his growth that omits
it is describing a different character.

## Other ancient traditions

The Epic Cycle's *Telegony* carried further traditions about his later
life, including a marriage to Circe. These are not in Homer and are not
merged into the account above.

## Later reception

Fénelon's *Les Aventures de Télémaque* (1699) turned him into the
subject of a treatise on the education of a prince and was, for a
century, one of the most widely read books in Europe. The word *mentor*
in modern English comes from the character Mentor in the Odyssey — whose
form Athena borrows — by way of Fénelon rather than directly from Homer.

## In modern adaptation

Adaptations tend to give him more of a relationship with his father than
the poem does, because the poem's version — a man and a son who have
never met, reunited in a hut, with the son immediately doubting — is
