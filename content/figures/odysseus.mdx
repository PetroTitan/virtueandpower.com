## In the Odyssey

The poem's first word about him is an adjective: *polytropos*, of many
turns. It can mean much-travelled or it can mean devious, and the poem
never chooses. His other standing epithets are *polymētis*, of many
counsels, and *polytlas*, much-enduring. All three describe capacities
rather than virtues.

What he does in the poem: he survives. He blinds a Cyclops by a trick
of language, resists a witch by a drug given him by a god, refuses
immortality, loses every one of his companions, comes home after twenty
years, conceals himself in his own house, endures being kicked and
struck by men eating his property, kills a hundred and eight of them,
and is then tested by his wife and fails to anticipate her test.

He is also a liar of extraordinary fluency. He tells at least four
elaborate false life stories in the second half of the poem — to
Athena, to Eumaeus, to Penelope, and finally to his own grieving
father. Athena, meeting him disguised and being lied to, laughs and
praises him for it. The poem does not present this as a flaw to be
corrected.

And he weeps: on Calypso's shore daily, at the Phaeacian court when
the bard sings about Troy, on being recognised by his son and his
servants. The Odyssey's hero cries more than most modern versions of
him are willing to show.

## In the Iliad

A different and smaller role. He is a counsellor and speaker, the man
sent on the embassy to Achilles in Book 9, and one of the two who
conduct the night raid of Book 10 — a book widely regarded as a late
addition to the poem. He is competent and valued and not central.

The two Homeric portraits are compatible but not identical, and the
Odyssey's interest in deception is much more developed.

## Other ancient traditions

Ancient opinion divided sharply, and the division is worth keeping in
view because modern readers often inherit one side of it without
knowing there was another.

The philosophical schools — the Cynics and later the Stoics — made him
a model: the man of endurance, self-sufficiency and reason, who resists
appetite in the form of the Lotus and the Sirens.

Attic tragedy did close to the opposite. Sophocles' *Philoctetes* has
him argue for lying to a wronged man; his *Ajax* frames him more
generously but as a political calculator. Euripides used him as a
sophistic operator. Latin literature, reading the war from the Trojan
side, inherited the hostile version: Virgil's Ulysses is *dirus*,
dread, and *scelerum inventor*, deviser of crimes.

The Epic Cycle's *Telegony* gave him a further and stranger end: a son
by Circe, Telegonus, arrives on Ithaca and kills him without knowing
who he is — a death "from the sea", which is one way of reading
Tiresias's prophecy in *Odyssey* 11.

## Later reception

Dante placed him in the eighth circle among the counsellors of fraud
and gave him a magnificent invented ending — a last voyage past the
straits of Gibraltar into the unknown ocean, and shipwreck. That voyage
is not in Homer at all, and it is the version Tennyson took up in
*Ulysses* (1842), where the restlessness became admirable.

Joyce's *Ulysses* (1922) made him an advertising canvasser in Dublin
and took the poem's structure as its scaffolding, which permanently
changed what the Odyssey was understood to be about.

## In modern adaptation

Screen versions have generally been drawn to the wanderings, which are
four books of twenty-four, and away from the disguise, the testing and
the lying, which are most of the rest. Christopher Nolan's 2026 film is
discussed at length elsewhere on this platform; our reading is that its
Odysseus is a more honourable and less deceptive figure than the poem's,
and that this is a coherent interpretive decision with real costs. See
<a href="/films/christopher-nolan-the-odyssey/characters">the film's characters</a>.

## What this page does not claim

figure existed. Not weak evidence — none. The name does not appear in
the Linear B tablets in a form that would settle anything, and no
