## In the Odyssey

Athena comes to her in a dream disguised as a friend and puts marriage
in her mind — which is how the laundry gets done. She asks her father
for a wagon, without mentioning marriage, and he grants it without
mentioning it either. The poem notices the tact on both sides.

At the river the girls wash, bathe, eat and play at ball. A shout wakes
Odysseus, who has been asleep under two olive bushes for two days after
nearly drowning. He comes out naked, filthy with brine, and the poem
compares him to a mountain lion. The other girls scatter. Nausicaa
stands still, because Athena puts courage in her — and, the poem is
careful to add, she is the only one who does.

He supplicates her from a distance, judging that grasping her knees in
his condition would offend, and delivers the most calculated speech in
Greek epic: he does not know whether she is a goddess or a mortal, she
is like Artemis, she is like a young palm shoot he once saw growing at
Delos. Only after all that does he ask for a rag to wear and directions
to the town.

She replies practically. She has him washed and clothed and fed, and
then gives him advice that determines the next two books: do not walk
into the city with me, because of what people will say about a woman
with a strange man; go to the palace; pass my father and supplicate my
mother.

## What the episode is

It is a test of judgement, and she passes it. A young woman meets a
large naked stranger in an isolated place, correctly assesses that he
is a shipwrecked suppliant rather than a threat, gives him exactly what
he needs and nothing that would compromise her, and manages her own
reputation while doing it.

The marriage the poem keeps raising never happens. Alcinous offers his
daughter in Book 7; Odysseus does not respond to the offer. Her last
words to him, in Book 8, are that he should remember her, because she
was the first to save his life. He answers that he will pray to her as
to a god all his days. Then he leaves.

## Other ancient traditions

Later sources sometimes marry her to Telemachus. That belongs to the
Cycle and to later mythography, not to this poem.

## Later reception

She has been a favourite of readers who find the poem's other women
constrained. Samuel Butler, whose 1900 prose translation is in the
public domain, argued in *The Authoress of the Odyssey* (1897) that the
poem was written by a woman and that Nausicaa was her self-portrait —
a thesis with essentially no scholarly support, but a sign of how the
character has struck people.

## In modern adaptation

The Phaeacian sequence is the poem's most reliably cut movement,
because it is a long stop that produces no fighting. Cutting it removes
the poem's model of perfect hospitality and its narrative frame for the
wanderings. The 2026 film omits it entirely. See
