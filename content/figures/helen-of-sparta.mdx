## In the Odyssey

She enters in Book 4 out of a scented chamber, with a silver work-basket
on wheels, and recognises Telemachus by his likeness to his father
before anyone has said who he is. When the company begins to weep she
puts a drug into the wine — a remedy from Egypt that stops grief and
anger for a day — and then tells a story.

The drug is the detail worth pausing on. Her method of managing the
past is chemical suspension, and she uses it before telling her version
of events. The poem puts those two facts side by side without comment.

Her story makes her Odysseus's secret ally inside Troy. Her husband's
immediately afterwards makes her the voice that nearly killed the Greeks
in the horse. The poem does not adjudicate.

In Book 15 she interprets an eagle carrying a goose as an omen of
Odysseus's return and vengeance, and is right.

## In the Iliad

A more painful portrait. She speaks of herself with contempt, calls
herself a cause of the war, and wishes she had died before it. She is
also treated with striking gentleness by Priam, who tells her he does
not blame her but the gods, and she is given the last lament over
Hector's body in the poem's final book.

Homer never describes her physically. The most famous statement of her
beauty is an effect rather than a description: the old men on the wall
watching her pass and agreeing, quietly, that it is no wonder men have
fought so long over such a woman — and then saying she should go home.

## The tradition that contradicts everything

There is a substantial ancient tradition in which Helen never went to
Troy. The poet Stesichorus is said to have been struck blind for
slandering her and to have recovered his sight after writing a
recantation denying that she ever sailed. Herodotus reports Egyptian
priests telling him she spent the war in Egypt. Euripides' *Helen*
dramatises it: a phantom went to Troy, the real woman waited in Egypt,
and the war was fought over an image.

This is not a variant detail. It is an alternative account of the
central event of Greek heroic myth, and it was current in antiquity
alongside Homer. This platform records it as a separate tradition and
does not merge it with the Homeric one.

## Cult

Helen received cult worship at Sparta and at Therapne. That is
documented religious practice, and it is a different order of fact from
any story about her.

## Later reception

Endlessly rewritten, generally as either the cause of the war or its
victim, and rarely as the ironic, capable and slightly frightening
woman of *Odyssey* 4.

## In modern adaptation

The 2026 film has one performer play both Helen and Clytemnestra, and
presents them as twin sisters. In the standard tradition the two are
daughters of Leda and sisters, but the genealogies are complicated and
disputed in antiquity, and calling them twins is a simplification the
sources do not straightforwardly support. Doubling them is a legitimate
theatrical device with a long stage history; the specific genealogical
claim is a separate matter, and is recorded in our
<a href="/films/christopher-nolan-the-odyssey/what-the-film-changed">ledger of changes</a>.

## What this page does not claim

No historical claim is made about any individual behind the figure. The
traditions are incompatible and are recorded separately rather than
harmonised.
