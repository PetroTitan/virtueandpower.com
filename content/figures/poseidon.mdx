## A note on the evidence level

Marked **documented**, and again the distinction is exact: the cult is
a historical fact and the stories are literature. A theonym
corresponding to Poseidon appears in the Linear B archives at Pylos,
where he seems to be among the principal recipients of offerings. That
is one of the strongest continuities between the Bronze Age religious
record and the world of the epics — and it is a continuity of worship,
not of narrative.

## In the Odyssey

He is the antagonist, and he is almost always offstage. The poem opens
with him away among the Ethiopians, which is why Athena can raise
Odysseus's case at all. He returns in Book 5, sees the raft, and raises
the storm that nearly kills its builder.

His grievance is specific and, within the poem's terms, legitimate:
Odysseus blinded his son. Polyphemus's prayer in Book 9 is the charter
for everything that follows — that Odysseus reach home late, in
another's ship, having lost all his companions, to find trouble in his
house. Every clause is fulfilled.

In Book 13 he complains to Zeus that the Phaeacians have conveyed
Odysseus home and that his honour is diminished. Zeus agrees he may act,
and Poseidon turns the returning ship to stone in sight of its own
harbour.

His anger is never resolved inside the poem. Tiresias's prophecy in
Book 11 requires Odysseus to travel inland with an oar until he meets
people who mistake it for a winnowing fan, and to sacrifice there. That
sacrifice lies beyond the poem's last line. The Odyssey ends with the
household restored and the sea-god still owed.

## What kind of god this is

Homeric gods are powers with interests, not moral exemplars. Poseidon
is not wicked and Athena is not good; they are on different sides, and
the poem's word for what is at stake is *timē*, honour as due standing.
Poseidon's objection in Book 13 is not that a criminal escaped but that
he will be held cheap among the gods.

Understanding this is necessary to read the poem's theology at all. A
reader who expects divine justice will find the Odyssey incoherent; a
reader who expects divine interest will find it consistent.

## Other ancient traditions

Beyond Homer he is the god of horses and of earthquakes as well as of
the sea — the Earth-Shaker — and the contest with Athena for Attica is
a well-known Athenian tradition that Homer does not tell.

## Later reception

Identified with the Roman Neptune, and thereafter increasingly a
personification of the sea rather than a power with a grudge.

## In modern adaptation

A film that declines to show gods acting must find another way to carry
Poseidon's pursuit, since the storm in Book 5 is his act and the whole
delay is his doing. How the 2026 film handles this is assessed at

## What this page does not claim

That Poseidon exists or does not. The cult is documented; the stories
are literary; the two are recorded separately.
