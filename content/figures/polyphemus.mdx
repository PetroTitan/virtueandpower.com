## In the Odyssey

The Cyclopes are introduced politically rather than physically: they
have no assemblies and no established rights, they do not plough or
plant, and each gives law to his own wife and children and takes no
account of the others. The poem's definition of savagery is the absence
of institutions.

Odysseus takes twelve men into the cave, against advice, because he
wants to see what its owner will give him — a guest-gift. Polyphemus
returns, rolls a stone across the entrance, and asks who they are.
Odysseus asks for hospitality in Zeus's name. Polyphemus replies that
the Cyclopes care nothing for Zeus, seizes two men, and eats them.

Asked for his name, Odysseus says *Outis* — Nobody. Polyphemus's
guest-gift, he announces, will be to eat Nobody last.

Odysseus gets him drunk on unmixed wine, and with four men drives a
sharpened and fire-hardened olive stake into his single eye. The other
Cyclopes, hearing him shout that Nobody is killing him by treachery,
conclude that he is ill and go away. In the morning the men escape tied
under the bellies of the rams; Odysseus goes last, under the great ram
that Polyphemus stops to talk to.

From the ship, and against the pleading of his crew, Odysseus shouts
his real name and patronymic. Polyphemus prays to his father Poseidon
that Odysseus may never reach home, or reach it late, in another's
ship, having lost all his companions, to find trouble in his house.
That prayer is the plot of the remaining fifteen books.

## The trick, and why it does not survive translation

The pun works twice over in Greek and not at all in English. *Outis*,
"nobody", is the name given; and *mē tis*, the other way of saying "no
one", is a homophone of *mētis*, cunning intelligence. The poem is
saying that Odysseus escapes by being nobody and by being cunning, in
the same breath, using the same sound.

This is why the Cyclops episode is the poem's purest demonstration of
what its hero is for, and why an adaptation that drops the name trick —
however spectacular its monster — has removed the reason the episode is
in the poem.

## Other ancient traditions

Euripides' satyr play *Cyclops* retells the episode for comedy. A
completely separate literary tradition, developed by Theocritus and
taken up by Ovid, makes Polyphemus a lovesick pastoral giant courting
the sea-nymph Galatea. That figure shares a name and nothing else, and
is not blended into the account above.

## Later reception

The episode is among the most frequently illustrated in Greek art from
an early date — the blinding appears on vases and on a well-known
seventh-century BCE amphora — and it has remained the single most
recognisable thing in the poem.

## In modern adaptation

The Cyclops is the sequence adaptations most reliably include and most
reliably reduce to spectacle, because the visual is easy and the
wordplay is impossible. See

## What this page does not claim

Polyphemus is a figure of poetry. The occasional suggestion that the
Cyclops myth originated in observation of fossil elephant skulls is a
modern hypothesis, not an established finding, and is not asserted here.
