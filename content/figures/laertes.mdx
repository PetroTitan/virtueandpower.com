## In the Odyssey

He is present in the poem long before he appears. Anticleia tells her
son among the dead in Book 11 that his father no longer comes to town,
sleeps in the ashes by the fire in winter and on fallen leaves in the
vineyard, and grieves. Penelope's shroud — the trick that holds the
suitors off for three years — is woven for him, which is why the excuse
is unrefusable.

He appears in Book 24, in his orchard, spading around a plant, in a
patched tunic and stitched leather leggings and a goatskin cap, looking
like a slave. Odysseus watches him and weeps.

And then, instead of revealing himself, he tests him. He invents
another false story — a man from Alybas, who entertained Odysseus five
years ago — and watches his father pour dust over his head and groan.
Only then does he say who he is, and prove it twice: by the scar, and
by naming the trees Laertes gave him when he was a boy following him
around the garden. Thirteen pear trees, ten apple, forty fig, and rows
of vines.

Athena then makes him taller and stronger, and in the fight that
follows he throws the spear that kills Eupeithes, the father of
Antinous. Three generations of the family stand in the field together
before Athena stops the fighting.

## Why the scene is there

The testing of Laertes is the most criticised thing Odysseus does in
the poem, including by readers who accept the massacre. There is no
strategic reason for it. The suitors are dead; the secrecy is over; the
old man is broken with grief.

The poem offers no justification and does not need one. It has spent
twenty-two books establishing that this is a man who cannot approach
anyone without a cover story, and Book 24 shows the habit outlasting
its usefulness. Whether that is characterisation or a defect of the
poem is one of the arguments about the authenticity of Book 24.

The recognition by the trees is, against that, the gentlest thing in
the poem. The proof is a boy walking through an orchard asking for the
names of things, remembered accurately forty years later.

## Other ancient traditions

Some later sources list him among the Argonauts. Homer does not.

## Later reception

Little independent life. He is a component of the Odysseus story rather
than a figure with his own tradition.

## In modern adaptation

He is the character adaptations cut most reliably, including the 2026
film, which omits him entirely. The reason is structural: he belongs to
a final movement that runs past the emotional climax of the marriage
bed, and film endings do not easily accommodate a second recognition
after the reunion. What is lost with him is the poem's three-generation
frame and the sense that a household is a thing that persists over
time rather than a couple. See
