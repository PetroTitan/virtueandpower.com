## In the Odyssey

She lives in a stone house in a forest clearing on Aeaea, with wolves
and lions around it that fawn instead of attacking. She is introduced by
genealogy: daughter of Helios the Sun and of the Oceanid Perse, and
sister of Aeetes — which places her in the family of the Argonautic
myth.

A scouting party under Eurylochus reaches the house and hears her
singing at a great loom. She feeds them a drugged mixture, strikes them
with a wand, and they become swine, keeping their human minds. Only
Eurylochus, who stayed outside suspecting a trap, escapes.

Odysseus goes to rescue them and is met by Hermes, who gives him the
herb *moly* — black at the root, white in flower, hard for mortals to
dig — and precise instructions: when she strikes at him, draw the sword
and rush at her as though to kill, and when she offers her bed, make her
swear a great oath that she will not harm him.

He does exactly this. The oath is sworn, the men are restored to human
form and made younger and taller than before, and the whole company
remains on Aeaea for a year until the crew urge departure. She then
tells Odysseus that he must go to the house of Hades to consult
Tiresias, and on his return she gives the sailing directions that get
him past the Sirens and through the strait.

## What the poem does and does not say

It is worth being exact, because this episode is rewritten more freely
than almost any other.

The poem does not say she hates men, or that she was wronged by
soldiers, or that her magic reveals a true nature rather than imposing a
false one — the swine keep their human minds and weep, and are restored
by an ointment. Those are modern additions, some of them very good ones,
but they are additions.

The poem also does not present the sexual relationship as
straightforwardly consensual or straightforwardly coerced. Hermes
instructs Odysseus to accept the bed in order to secure her help, and to
extract an oath first. The oath is a safety mechanism, not a courtship.
The poem gives the arrangement a year and then moves on without
comment.

## Other ancient traditions

The *Telegony* of the Epic Cycle made her the mother of Odysseus's son
Telegonus, who eventually kills his father without knowing him. Ovid's
*Metamorphoses* added the transformations of Picus and of Scylla. Neither
is Homeric, and neither is folded into the account above.

## Later reception

She was allegorised early and persistently as appetite: the enchantress
who reduces men to beasts, read as pleasure degrading reason. That
reading is ancient and durable and is not what the text says — her
victims are not represented as having deserved it.

Modern fiction has reversed the polarity. Madeline Miller's *Circe*
(2018) narrates her life from the inside and made her, for a large
readership, a figure of exile and self-possession rather than of
temptation.

## In modern adaptation

The 2026 film's Circe follows the modern reworking rather than the
Homeric text: an isolated woman transforming men out of fear, whose
magic is presented as exposing what they are. We regard this as a
defensible interpretation with a clear literary lineage, and as a
change from the poem rather than a rendering of it. See
