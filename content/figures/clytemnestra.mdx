## In the Odyssey

She is present in the poem only as a warning, and the poem is hard on
her. Agamemnon in Book 11 describes her as the woman who plotted his
death, who killed Cassandra beside him, and who would not close his eyes
or shut his mouth as he died. He generalises from her to women in
general, and then — this is the poem's characteristic move — immediately
exempts Penelope.

That structure is the point. Clytemnestra exists in the Odyssey to
define Penelope by contrast. Nestor's version in Book 3 adds a detail
that sharpens it: Agamemnon left a bard behind to watch over her, and
she resisted Aegisthus for a long time until Aegisthus marooned the bard
on a desert island. Even Homer's hostile account concedes she held out.

In Book 24 Agamemnon's shade completes the antithesis: Penelope's fame
will never die, and a song of loathing will attend Clytemnestra.

## What Homer does not give her

He does not give her the sacrifice of Iphigenia. That story is in the
Cycle and in tragedy, and it is what converts her from a faithless wife
into a mother with a grievance.

He does not make her the killer. In the Odyssey the deed is Aegisthus's;
she is complicit and is blamed, but the sword is his.

He does not give her a speech. The Homeric Clytemnestra never speaks.

## The tragic figure

Aeschylus's *Agamemnon* (458 BCE) is a different work of art with a
different character. There she kills her husband with her own hands,
declares it, and defends it in some of the most formidable verse in
Greek. The motive is Iphigenia. The consequence is the *Oresteia*, and
a trial, and the transformation of the Furies.

Anyone who thinks of Clytemnestra as a figure of terrible authority is
thinking of Aeschylus. Homer's is a footnote in someone else's warning.

## Later reception

The tragic version has dominated ever since and has been repeatedly
reclaimed in modern retellings.

## In modern adaptation

The 2026 film has one performer play both Clytemnestra and Helen and
presents them as twin sisters. The two are sisters in the standard
tradition — daughters of Leda — but the genealogies are contested in
antiquity and the specific claim of twinship is a simplification. The
doubling itself is a theatrical device with a long and respectable
history. Both points are recorded in our
<a href="/films/christopher-nolan-the-odyssey/what-the-film-changed">ledger of changes</a>.

## What this page does not claim

No historical claim is made. The Homeric and tragic versions are
recorded separately and are not merged.
