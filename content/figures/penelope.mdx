## In the Odyssey

She holds an impossible position for twenty years and holds it by
intelligence. Her husband is missing, presumed dead by nearly everyone;
her son is a child and then a young man without authority; her father
and brothers press her to remarry; and a hundred and eight men of the
regional aristocracy are living in her house, eating its resources, on
the understanding that she will eventually pick one.

Her principal defence is the shroud. She announces that she will
choose when she has finished weaving a burial cloth for her
father-in-law Laertes, and unpicks by night what she weaves by day. It
works for three years and fails only when one of her own women betrays
her. The poem tells this three times, from three different mouths, with
different emphasis each time.

It is worth being precise about what the trick is. It is not passivity
and it is not merely delay. It is the deployment of an obligation the
suitors cannot refuse — the duty owed to the dead — as a weapon against
them. That is *mētis*, the same faculty the poem gives her husband.

In Book 18 she comes down before the suitors, reproaches them for
courting without gifts, and extracts presents from the men consuming
her house. In Book 19 she interviews the disguised beggar at length and
weeps at his account of her husband's clothing. Immediately afterwards
she announces the contest of the bow — a contest only Odysseus can
win, declared the moment a stranger has told her that Odysseus is near.

And in Book 23 she refuses to accept the man who has just killed a
hundred and eight people in her hall until she has tested him. She
orders the marriage bed moved out of the chamber. He explodes, because
he built it around a living olive tree rooted in the earth and it
cannot be moved. That is the only moment in the poem where Odysseus is
outmanoeuvred, and his wife does it.

## The question of what she knows

Whether Penelope recognises her husband before Book 23 is among the
oldest unresolved questions in Homeric criticism, and this platform does
not pretend to settle it.

The case that she does: the timing of the contest is extraordinary, and
the contest is precisely designed for one man. The case that she does
not: the poem shows her genuinely uncertain in Book 23, and Athena is
repeatedly described as directing her attention. A third position holds
that the poem deliberately leaves it undecidable, and that the
undecidability is the characterisation.

Adaptations cannot leave it open. They must show something, and what
they show is one of the most consequential interpretive choices
available to them.

## Other ancient traditions

Homer's Penelope is not the only one. A later mythographic strand — at
odds with the Odyssey and with itself — makes her the mother of the god
Pan, in some versions fathered by Hermes and in others by the suitors
collectively. Some versions have Odysseus repudiate or kill her on his
return.

This platform records that tradition and does not blend it with the
Homeric one. They are incompatible accounts, and merging them produces
a biography that no ancient source contains.

## Later reception

She became the type of the faithful wife, which flattens her, and the
type of the passive waiting woman, which is worse and is not in the
text. Ovid's *Heroides* 1 gives her a letter. Twentieth- and
twenty-first-century reworkings have generally been concerned to give
her back the agency the type removed — Margaret Atwood's *The
Penelopiad* (2005) narrates the poem from her side and from the side of
the twelve hanged maids.

The recurring problem is that her intelligence is expressed through
waiting, and waiting is hard to film. Adaptations tend either to reduce

## What this page does not claim

Nothing here is a historical claim. Penelope is a character in a poem,
and the traditions about her contradict each other.
