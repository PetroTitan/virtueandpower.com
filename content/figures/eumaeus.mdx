## In the Odyssey

He runs the pig farm, and he is a slave — *dmōs*, a household
dependant, bought by Laertes. He is not blind, and he is not a tutor;
he is a herdsman with twelve sties, four assistants and a dog problem.

Athena sends the disguised Odysseus to him first. What follows is the
poem's benchmark of hospitality, performed by its poorest host: he
drives off his own dogs, brings the stranger in, spreads brushwood and
a goatskin, kills two young pigs — apologising that the good animals go
to the suitors — pours a libation, and states the principle plainly,
that all strangers and beggars are from Zeus and a small gift given
kindly is a gift.

He is also the only person in the poem the narrator addresses directly
in the second person. Why Homer does this for a swineherd has been
discussed for centuries without agreement.

In Book 15 he tells his own story. He was the son of a king on an
island called Syrie. A Phoenician woman working in his father's house —
herself kidnapped and sold — took him aboard a trading ship as a child.
Laertes bought him. He was raised in the household alongside
Odysseus's sister.

He is admitted to the plot in Book 21, shown the scar, told to bar the
doors, and fights in the hall in Book 22.

## What the poem says through him

Eumaeus is where the Odyssey is most direct about slavery. He states
that Zeus takes away half a man's worth on the day he becomes a slave.
He is describing himself, and he says it while performing his duties
faithfully.

The poem does not present this as a contradiction, and modern readers
should not resolve it by choosing one half. The household of the
Odyssey runs on owned people; some of them are treated well; the poem
records both the treatment and the ownership, and lets Eumaeus say what
it costs.

His reward, when it comes, is stated as payment: a wife, property near
the house, and standing as a brother to Telemachus. The poem is
unsentimental about the transaction.

## Other ancient traditions

None of substance. He is a Homeric creation without an independent
tradition.

## Later reception

He became a type of the faithful servant, which loses the fact that he
is a slave and that the poem says what that means.

## In modern adaptation

The 2026 film casts him as blind and as Odysseus's tutor. Neither is in
Homer: the poem's Eumaeus is a working herdsman with sight, and the
blindness appears to import the traditional attribute of the bard. We
treat this as an invention rather than an interpretation. See the
<a href="/films/christopher-nolan-the-odyssey/what-the-film-changed">ledger of changes</a>.
