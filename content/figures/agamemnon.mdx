## In the Odyssey

He is a structural device as much as a character. His story is told or
referred to in Books 1, 3, 4, 11 and 24, by a different speaker each
time, and its function is constant: this is what happens when a man
comes home without taking precautions.

Zeus invokes it in the poem's first divine speech, as an example of
mortals bringing ruin on themselves against warning — Aegisthus was
told by the gods not to take Agamemnon's wife or kill him, and did
both. Nestor tells it at length to Telemachus in Book 3 and holds up
Orestes' vengeance as a model. Menelaus learns of it from Proteus in
Book 4.

In Book 11 Agamemnon tells it himself, among the dead: killed at a
feast, like an ox at the manger, his men slaughtered around him, and
his wife not even closing his eyes as he died. He then gives Odysseus
the advice that shapes the entire second half of the poem — come home
secretly, and do not trust your wife.

Odysseus follows it. The disguise, the testing of the household, the
withheld identity: all of it is a response to a warning from a dead
king.

In Book 24 he speaks again, hears the suitors' account, and pronounces
Penelope's fame imperishable, contrasting her explicitly with his own
wife.

## Homer's version is not the tragedians' version

This distinction matters and is routinely lost.

In Homer, Aegisthus is the principal agent — the seducer who takes the
kingdom and sets the ambush. Clytemnestra is complicit and is treated
with contempt, but the killing is Aegisthus's crime, and Orestes' act of
vengeance is presented as simply admirable, with no Furies and no
trial.

In Aeschylus, three centuries later, Clytemnestra strikes the blow
herself, her motive is the sacrifice of Iphigenia at Aulis, and the
consequences run through two further plays. The sacrifice of Iphigenia
is not narrated in either Homeric poem.

Readers who arrive at Homer through Aeschylus routinely read the tragic
version back into the epic. It is not there.

## Later reception

The Oresteia made his death one of the foundational stories of European
drama. The gold mask excavated by Schliemann at Mycenae and named the
"Mask of Agamemnon" is centuries too early for any traditional dating of
the Trojan War; the name is a nineteenth-century label, not an
identification.
