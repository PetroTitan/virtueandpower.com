## What Mycenaean Greece was

The Late Bronze Age Aegean, roughly the fifteenth to twelfth centuries
BCE, held a network of palace centres — Mycenae, Pylos, Tiryns, Thebes,
Knossos on Crete — with monumental architecture, fortification walls of
enormous unworked blocks, shaft graves and tholos tombs, frescoed
halls, and long-distance trade reaching Egypt and the Levant.

The decisive evidence is documentary. The Linear B tablets, deciphered
by Michael Ventris with John Chadwick in 1952, are administrative
records in an early form of Greek. They were preserved by accident:
unfired clay, baked hard in the fires that destroyed the palaces.

## What the poem describes instead

The Odyssey's world has none of the bureaucracy. There is no writing in
it — the single apparent reference to written signs, the folded tablet
in the Bellerophon story in the *Iliad*, is famously obscure and is not
literacy in the Linear B sense. There are no scribes, no inventories,
no redistribution, no officials.

Authority is personal. A *basileus* leads because he is well-born,
wealthy, capable and listened to. Wealth is held as treasure and
livestock and moved by gift-exchange rather than by administration.
The household, not the palace, is the unit.

## What does survive

Some things do carry across, and they are worth naming precisely,
because they are the reason the poem cannot simply be dismissed as
having no Bronze Age content.

<EvidenceClaim
  level="documented"
  claim="The boar's-tusk helmet described in the Iliad matches excavated Mycenaean examples."
  detail="Helmets constructed from split boar's tusks sewn to a leather cap have been excavated from Mycenaean contexts. The description at Iliad 10.261-265 corresponds closely. Note that Iliad 10 is widely held to be among the latest material in that poem, which complicates any simple account of continuous transmission."
  sources="Iliad 10.261-265; excavated examples, National Archaeological Museum, Athens"
/>

<EvidenceClaim
  level="documented"
  claim="Personal names later borne by Homeric characters occur in Linear B as ordinary names."
  detail="Forms corresponding to Achilles and Hector appear in the tablets as the names of unremarkable individuals. This shows the names are old. It does not show the characters are."
  sources="Linear B tablets, Knossos and Pylos"
/>

<EvidenceClaim
  level="probable"
  claim="The megaron hall plan of the poem broadly reflects Mycenaean palace architecture."
  detail="The poem's great hall with a central hearth and a throne is compatible with the excavated megaron at Pylos and elsewhere. The correspondence is general rather than detailed, and the poem's halls are also doing narrative work."
  sources="Excavated megara at Pylos, Mycenae and Tiryns"
/>

## The collapse in between

Between the palaces and the poem lies the destruction of the Mycenaean
system around 1200 to 1150 BCE, part of a wider disruption across the
eastern Mediterranean. The palaces burned, the administrative system
ended, Linear B ceased to be written, monumental building stopped, and
population and material complexity fell sharply.

<EvidenceClaim
  level="disputed"
  claim="The causes of the Late Bronze Age collapse."
  detail="Candidate explanations include migration and raiding, systems collapse in an over-specialised trade network, drought and climate stress, earthquake, internal revolt, and combinations of these. The Egyptian records of the groups conventionally called the Sea Peoples are part of the evidence and are themselves difficult. No single explanation is established."
  sources="Egyptian records at Medinet Habu; excavation across the eastern Mediterranean"
/>

The gap between the palaces and the poem is therefore something like
four hundred years, spanning a period of sharply reduced material
complexity. Anything genuinely transmitted across it came through oral
tradition, and oral tradition preserves phrases far better than it
preserves institutions.

## The practical conclusion

The Odyssey is not a description of Mycenaean Greece and should never
be used as one. It preserves some Bronze Age furniture inside a social
world that is not Bronze Age, narrated in a language older than either.

This matters directly for judging screen adaptations. A film set in a
recognisably Mycenaean material world would be departing from Homer; a
film that follows Homer's material descriptions will contain
anachronisms, because Homer does. See
<a href="/films/christopher-nolan-the-odyssey/costumes-and-material-culture">costumes and material culture</a>.
