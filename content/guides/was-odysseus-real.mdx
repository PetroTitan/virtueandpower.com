## The short answer

<EvidenceClaim
  level="unknown"
  claim="Whether a historical individual stood behind the figure of Odysseus."
  detail="There is no evidence bearing on the question in either direction. This is not a case of weak or contested evidence; there is none of any kind."
  sources="No evidence available"
/>

That is the whole of the honest answer, and the rest of this guide
explains why it is the right one and why it is more interesting than it
sounds.

## What would count as evidence

It is worth being concrete about what would actually settle this, because
the question is usually asked without anyone specifying what an answer
would look like.

**A contemporary document naming him.** For a Late Bronze Age figure
this would mean a Linear B tablet or a Hittite or Egyptian record. The
Linear B corpus is administrative and mentions many ordinary people; it
does not mention anyone identifiable as this figure.

**An inscription.** Greek alphabetic writing begins around the eighth
century BCE, centuries after any traditional date for the Trojan War.
There is nothing to inscribe him on.

**A burial.** Even if a royal burial were excavated on Ithaca, nothing
about it could attach a name. This is the point Schliemann's "Mask of
Agamemnon" illustrates: a genuine and spectacular Bronze Age object,
named by a nineteenth-century excavator, dating centuries earlier than
any traditional date for the war, and evidence for nothing about
Agamemnon.

None of these exists. None is likely to.

## Why the names in Linear B do not help

The tablets contain personal names corresponding to Achilles and
Hector, borne by ordinary people. This is genuinely interesting: it
shows the names are old and Greek, and not invented by the poets.

It shows nothing about the characters. A medieval English tax roll
listing several men named Arthur is not evidence for the round table.
The most that can be inferred is that the epic tradition drew its names
from a real Greek onomastic stock, which is what one would expect
either way.

## Why this differs from the question about Troy

The two questions are often run together and should not be.

For Troy there is a place, a long occupation sequence, destruction
levels, and a set of Hittite documents whose names may or may not
correspond. The evidence is real and its interpretation is contested —
which is why our page on the Trojan War carries claims labelled
probable and disputed rather than unknown.

For Odysseus there is nothing to interpret. Places can be excavated;
individuals in a preliterate society cannot be recovered unless
somebody wrote their name down.

## Why the honest answer is not disappointing

The instinct that a story matters more if it happened is worth
resisting, and the Greeks did not share it in the form we do. They had
a category — the age of heroes — that was neither history nor fiction
as we use those words: a real past, populated by beings greater than
present people, at a distance that made verification neither possible
nor interesting.

The Odyssey's achievement does not depend on a man having existed.
Nothing about the test of the bed, or the widow simile in Book 8, or
the twelve hanged women, becomes better if someone can be dug up.
