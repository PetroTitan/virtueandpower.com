## The problem

Odysseus describes his home in Book 9. The description is short,
specific, and does not fit.

He says Ithaca is low-lying, and that it lies furthest out toward the
dark or the west, with the other islands apart toward the dawn and the
sun. He names neighbours: Dulichium, Same, wooded Zacynthus.

Modern Ithaki is mountainous rather than low-lying, and it is not the
westernmost of the Ionian group — Cephalonia lies west of it. Zacynthus
is identifiable with modern Zakynthos, Same plausibly with Same on
Cephalonia; Dulichium has never been securely identified at all.

## The possible resolutions

There are four, and they are not equally weighted.

**The poet was describing somewhere else.** This is the relocation
hypothesis, of which the best-known modern version is Robert
Bittlestone's proposal in *Odysseus Unbound* (2005) that the Paliki
peninsula of western Cephalonia was an island in antiquity, separated
by a channel since filled by seismic activity, and that it was Homer's
Ithaca.

<EvidenceClaim
  level="disputed"
  claim="The Paliki peninsula of Cephalonia was Homer's Ithaca."
  detail="The hypothesis fits the poem's geography well and has prompted genuine geological investigation into whether a marine channel existed and could have been infilled. It has not been accepted by any consensus of classicists or geologists, and the required volume and timing of infill remain contested. It is a serious proposal with an unresolved status, not an established finding."
  sources="R. Bittlestone, Odysseus Unbound, 2005, and subsequent geological survey"
/>

**The poet did not know the region well.** An entirely ordinary
explanation. A poet composing in Ionia, working in a tradition, may
have inherited names without accurate geography. The Odyssey is
similarly vague about a great deal else.

**The directional words mean something other than what is assumed.**
The Greek adverbs translated west and east are argued over, and readings
that make the description consistent have been proposed since antiquity.

**The description is not meant as topography.** A poem may place a home
at the edge of the world, furthest toward the dark, for reasons that
have nothing to do with charts.

## What has actually been found on Ithaki

The archaeology of the modern island is real, and it is worth
separating from the identification question.

<EvidenceClaim
  level="documented"
  claim="A cult site with rich early offerings existed at the Polis cave on Ithaki."
  detail="Excavation in the 1930s recovered a group of bronze tripod-cauldrons dating to roughly the ninth and eighth centuries BCE. A later inscribed fragment records a vow to Odysseus, establishing that he was venerated at the site in the historical period."
  sources="Excavations at the Polis cave, Ithaki"
/>

The tripods have attracted attention because Odysseus receives tripods
as guest-gifts from the Phaeacians, and the counts have sometimes been
matched up. That correspondence is suggestive and is not proof of
anything: tripods were a standard high-value dedication across the
Greek world, and a cult of a hero at a place named in an epic is
evidence about the epic's reception, not about the epic's accuracy.

<EvidenceClaim
  level="probable"
  claim="People in historical Greece believed modern Ithaki was Odysseus's Ithaca."
  detail="The cult evidence and the continuity of the name support this. What ancient Greeks believed about the location is a well-evidenced historical fact, and is independent of whether the belief was correct."
  sources="Polis cave cult material; ancient literary references"
/>

<EvidenceClaim
  level="documented"
  claim="Mycenaean-period settlement existed on Ithaki."
  detail="Sites on the north of the island, including the location traditionally called the School of Homer, have produced Late Bronze Age material. This establishes occupation. It attaches to no individual and to no palace of the kind the poem describes."
  sources="Excavation reports, northern Ithaki"
/>

## The honest summary

The island called Ithaca today was called Ithaca in antiquity, was
occupied in the Bronze Age, and had a hero cult of Odysseus in the
historical period. Whether it is the island the poem describes is
undecided, because the poem's own description does not fit it and the
best alternative proposal is unproven.

That is a genuinely interesting result, and it is not improved by
tidying.
