## What is being asked

"Did the Trojan War happen?" bundles several separable questions:

- Was there a place the Greeks called Ilios, and where was it?
- Was it destroyed, and when, and by whom?
- Was there a coalition expedition from mainland Greece against it?
- Did it last ten years, and was it fought over a woman, and did the
  people named in the poems take part?

The evidence is quite different for each, and confidence should be too.

## The destructions

<EvidenceClaim
  level="documented"
  claim="Hisarlik was destroyed and rebuilt repeatedly across its occupation."
  detail="The excavated sequence contains multiple destruction horizons. This is not in question."
  sources="Excavation reports, Troy I-IX"

<EvidenceClaim
  level="disputed"
  claim="A particular destruction level corresponds to the war the Greeks remembered."
  detail="Troy VI and Troy VIIa have both been argued for. Destruction by earthquake, by fire and by human attack are not always distinguishable in the record, and even where attack is likely, nothing connects it to a coalition of Greek kings or to any named person. The competing position is that a memory of Late Bronze Age conflict in the region was absorbed into a heroic tradition which then generated its own detail."
  sources="Excavation reports on Troy VI and VIIa; continuing debate"
/>

This is the point at which most popular accounts go wrong. The finding
that Hisarlik burned is regularly reported as confirming the *Iliad*.
It confirms that a well-placed fortified site in a contested region met
the fate such sites regularly met.

## The Hittite evidence

<EvidenceClaim
  level="disputed"
  claim="Hittite texts referring to Ahhiyawa and Wilusa refer to the Achaeans and to Ilios."
  detail="The linguistic correspondences are suggestive and many specialists accept them. What Ahhiyawa denoted politically — a single kingdom, a region, a shifting label for Aegean Greeks — is contested. The texts describe diplomatic friction and intermittent conflict over a long period, not a single war. Those who reject the equations note that a correspondence of names does not establish a correspondence of the entities named."
  sources="Hittite archives from Hattusa, including the Tawagalawa and Alaksandu material"
/>

The Alaksandu treaty, concerning a ruler of Wilusa, has attracted
attention because the name resembles Alexandros, the alternative name
of Paris. The resemblance is real. What follows from it is disputed,
and it is not a licence to treat the poem's cast as historical.

## What archaeology can and cannot do

Archaeology is good at establishing occupation, destruction,
rebuilding, trade and fortification. It is poor at establishing who
destroyed something and why. It is nearly powerless on whether a named
individual existed.

A burnt layer is compatible with a great many stories, including the
story that there was no expedition at all and that a local conflict was
absorbed into a tradition four centuries later.

## The war in the poems is not one war

A further point that is frequently lost. The *Iliad* covers a few weeks
of a tenth year and does not narrate the fall of the city. The wooden
horse is referred to twice in the *Odyssey* and narrated at length
nowhere in Homer; the extended account most readers know comes from the
Epic Cycle and from *Aeneid* 2. The judgement of Paris, the sacrifice
of Iphigenia, the death of Achilles and the sack itself all belong to
other poems.

"The Trojan War" as a single connected story is a construction of the
whole tradition, not the content of either Homeric poem. See
<a href="/homer/works">the works attributed to Homer</a>.
